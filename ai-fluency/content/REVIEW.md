## AI fundamentals (30)

### Core concepts

#### Machine learning _(also: ML)_

- [ ] Approved

> Needs a better source: the cited page is a general course, not a direct definition of this term.

**Plain English.** Instead of writing rules by hand, you show a computer lots of examples and it learns the pattern itself. A spam filter learns what spam looks like from millions of emails people marked as spam.

**Under the hood.** A model is a math function with adjustable settings. Training adjusts those settings until the model's outputs match the examples well. Supervised learning uses labeled examples, unsupervised learning finds structure in unlabeled data, and reinforcement learning learns from rewards.

**Customer line.** "Machine learning means the system learned from examples rather than following rules someone wrote, so it can handle cases nobody spelled out in advance."

**Follow-up:** _So is ML the same thing as AI?_ ML is the main way modern AI gets built, but AI is the broader goal of machines doing tasks that normally need human judgment. Today, almost every AI product you hear about is built with machine learning.

**Myth:** Machine learning systems keep learning on their own every time you use them. **Fact:** Most deployed models are frozen after training. They only get better when the vendor retrains or updates them.

Sources: [Microsoft Learn: Introduction to generative AI and agents](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/training/modules/fundamentals-generative-ai/); [DeepLearning.AI: Generative AI for Everyone](https://www.deeplearning.ai/courses/generative-ai-for-everyone)

#### Generative AI _(also: GenAI)_

- [ ] Approved

**Plain English.** AI that creates new content (text, images, code, audio) instead of only sorting or scoring existing content. ChatGPT and Claude are generative AI.

**Under the hood.** Generative models learn the patterns in their training data well enough to produce new examples that fit those patterns. Traditional or predictive AI classifies or forecasts (spam or not spam, likely to churn or not), while generative AI produces an output like a paragraph or an image.

**Customer line.** "Traditional AI answers 'which bucket does this go in?' Generative AI answers 'write me the first draft.'"

**Follow-up:** _Is generative AI accurate enough to trust?_ It's great at drafts, summaries, and first passes, but it can state wrong things confidently. The best setups keep a person reviewing anything important and connect the model to trusted data.

**Myth:** Generative AI looks up answers in a database, like a search engine. **Fact:** By default it generates text from patterns it learned in training. It only 'looks things up' if the product connects it to a search tool or your data.

Sources: [Microsoft Learn: Introduction to generative AI and agents](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/training/modules/fundamentals-generative-ai/); [DeepLearning.AI: Generative AI for Everyone](https://www.deeplearning.ai/courses/generative-ai-for-everyone)

#### Large language model _(also: LLM)_

- [ ] Approved

**Plain English.** The engine behind chat assistants like Claude and ChatGPT. It's a huge model trained on a massive amount of text, so it can write, summarize, answer questions, and follow instructions.

**Under the hood.** An LLM is a neural network with billions of parameters, pretrained to predict the next token in text and then fine-tuned (often with RLHF) to follow instructions and act like a helpful assistant.

**Customer line.** "An LLM is the language engine. Our product is the car built around it: your data, your workflows, and the guardrails."

**Follow-up:** _Which LLM do you use?_ Be ready with the real answer from your product team. It's also worth explaining why that model was chosen (quality, cost, speed, data terms) and whether the product can switch models later.

**Myth:** An LLM understands language the way a person does. **Fact:** It's extremely good at predicting useful text from patterns. Whether that counts as 'understanding' is debated, which is why it can sound sure while being wrong.

Sources: [Anthropic glossary: LLM](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/glossary)

#### Foundation model

- [ ] Approved

> Needs a better source: the cited page is a general course, not a direct definition of this term.

**Plain English.** A big general-purpose model that lots of different products are built on top of, like a base layer. Claude, GPT, and Gemini are foundation models.

**Under the hood.** Foundation models are trained once at enormous cost on broad data, then adapted for many tasks through prompting, retrieval, or fine-tuning. Most AI startups don't train their own. They build on a foundation model from a provider like Anthropic, OpenAI, or Google.

**Customer line.** "Like most AI products, we build on a foundation model and add what makes it useful for your team: your data, the workflow, and the controls."

**Follow-up:** _If you're built on someone else's model, what stops me from just using that model directly?_ Nothing stops you from trying. The value is in everything around the model: the integrations, the security and admin controls, the workflow, and the support. That's the honest build vs buy conversation.

**Myth:** Every AI company trains its own model from scratch. **Fact:** Very few do. Most build products on top of models from a handful of providers.

Sources: [Microsoft Learn: Introduction to generative AI and agents](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/training/modules/fundamentals-generative-ai/)

#### Parameters _(also: weights)_

- [ ] Approved

**Plain English.** The millions or billions of internal settings a model adjusts while it learns. They're where the model's 'knowledge' is stored.

**Under the hood.** Parameters (also called weights) are the numbers inside a neural network that training tunes. Parameter count is a rough measure of model size. More parameters can mean more capability, but also higher cost and slower responses.

**Customer line.** "Parameters are the model's internal dials. Bigger isn't automatically better for you. The right model is the one that's accurate enough at a cost and speed that fit the job."

**Follow-up:** _Should I pick the vendor with the biggest model?_ Not by size alone. Ask how it performs on tasks like yours, how fast it responds, and what it costs at your volume.

**Myth:** A model with more parameters is always smarter. **Fact:** Training data, training methods, and fine-tuning matter a lot. Smaller newer models often beat larger older ones.

Sources: [Anthropic glossary: LLM](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/glossary)

#### Training vs inference

- [ ] Approved

**Plain English.** Training is when the model learns, which happens once, up front, and is expensive. Inference is when you use it: every time you ask a question and get an answer.

**Under the hood.** Training adjusts the model's parameters using large datasets and lots of computing power. Inference runs the finished, frozen model on new input to produce an output. Usage-based AI pricing is mostly paying for inference.

**Customer line.** "Training is building the brain. Inference is the brain answering your question. When you use the product, you're doing inference, and your prompts don't retrain the model."

**Follow-up:** _So does my data end up training the model when I use it?_ Using the model (inference) doesn't change it by itself. Whether the vendor later uses your data for training is a policy question, so check the contract and data terms.

**Myth:** Every question you ask an AI immediately teaches it something new. **Fact:** Inference doesn't change the model. Any learning from your data would happen only if the vendor chooses to retrain on it later.

Sources: [Anthropic glossary: Pretraining](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/glossary); [Microsoft Learn: Introduction to generative AI and agents](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/training/modules/fundamentals-generative-ai/)

#### Multimodal

- [ ] Approved

> Needs a better source: the cited page is a general course, not a direct definition of this term.

**Plain English.** AI that works with more than one kind of input or output: text plus images, audio, video, or documents like PDFs.

**Under the hood.** Multimodal models process several data types in one model. For example, they can read a screenshot or chart and answer questions about it. Support varies: a model might read images but not create them.

**Customer line.** "Multimodal means you can hand the AI a screenshot, a PDF, or a photo, not just typed text."

**Follow-up:** _Can it read our scanned documents and charts?_ Often yes, but accuracy on messy scans and complex charts varies. It's worth testing with a few of your real documents.

**Myth:** If a model can read images, it can also generate them. **Fact:** Reading and creating are separate abilities. Many models do one but not the other.

Sources: [Microsoft Learn: Introduction to generative AI and agents](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/training/modules/fundamentals-generative-ai/)

#### Open-weight vs closed models _(also: open source models)_

- [ ] Approved

> Needs a better source: the cited page is a general course, not a direct definition of this term.

**Plain English.** Open-weight models can be downloaded and run on your own servers. Closed models, like Claude or GPT, are used only through the provider's service.

**Under the hood.** With open weights (for example, Llama or Mistral models), the trained parameters are published, so you can self-host and customize them, but you own the hosting, scaling, and safety work. Closed models are accessed by API, where the provider handles infrastructure and updates. 'Open weight' isn't always 'open source,' since the training data and code may not be released.

**Customer line.** "Open-weight gives you maximum control but you run everything. Closed models are managed for you and are often the most capable. It's a classic control vs convenience tradeoff."

**Follow-up:** _Isn't self-hosting an open model more secure?_ It can keep data fully in-house, but security then depends on your own team. Many enterprises get similar assurance from a provider's data terms, regional hosting, and certifications.

**Myth:** Open-weight models are always free to run. **Fact:** The download may be free, but the servers, GPUs, and staff to run them are not.

Sources: [Microsoft Learn: Introduction to generative AI and agents](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/training/modules/fundamentals-generative-ai/)

### How models are made

#### Pretraining

- [ ] Approved

**Plain English.** The first, huge phase of building an LLM, where it reads an enormous amount of text and learns to predict the next word. That's where it picks up language, facts, and reasoning patterns.

**Under the hood.** Pretraining uses a large, mostly unlabeled body of text. The result is good at continuing text but not at following instructions, which is why fine-tuning and RLHF come after it.

**Customer line.** "Pretraining is where the model learns general knowledge. Everything after that is about making it helpful, safe, and good at specific jobs."

**Follow-up:** _Does that mean the model knows about events from last week?_ Not on its own. Its knowledge stops at a training cutoff date. For current information it needs a search tool or access to live data.

**Myth:** A pretrained model is ready to be a helpful chatbot. **Fact:** A raw pretrained model just continues text. It takes fine-tuning and RLHF to make it follow instructions.

Sources: [Anthropic glossary: Pretraining](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/glossary)

#### Fine-tuning

- [ ] Approved

**Plain English.** Taking a model that's already trained and training it a bit more on a specific set of examples, so it gets better at one domain, task, or style.

**Under the hood.** Fine-tuning further trains a pretrained model on additional data, shifting its behavior toward the patterns in that data. It's slower and pricier to change than prompting or RAG, and the quality of the fine-tuning data matters a lot.

**Customer line.** "Fine-tuning changes the model itself. For most business use cases, connecting the model to your data (RAG) gets you there faster and is easier to keep up to date."

**Follow-up:** _Will you fine-tune a model on our data?_ Ask what problem fine-tuning would solve. If it's 'the model needs to know our documents,' retrieval is usually better. Fine-tuning fits better for a specific format, tone, or narrow task.

**Myth:** Fine-tuning is the best way to teach a model your company's facts. **Fact:** Facts change, and a fine-tuned model goes stale. RAG pulls in your current documents at question time instead.

Sources: [Anthropic glossary: Fine-tuning](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/glossary)

#### RLHF (Reinforcement learning from human feedback) _(also: RLHF)_

- [ ] Approved

**Plain English.** A training step where people rank the model's answers from better to worse, and the model learns to give more answers like the good ones.

**Under the hood.** Human raters compare two or more model outputs. Reinforcement learning then pushes the model toward outputs like the higher-ranked ones. It's a big part of what turns a raw pretrained model into a helpful assistant.

**Customer line.** "RLHF is how models learn manners and judgment: humans grade the answers and the model learns what 'good' looks like."

**Follow-up:** _Does that make the model unbiased?_ No. It reflects the preferences of the people and guidelines that shaped it. It helps a lot, but it isn't a guarantee.

**Myth:** RLHF means humans review every answer the AI gives you. **Fact:** The human feedback happens during training. Your live answers aren't reviewed by a person unless the product adds a human-in-the-loop step.

Sources: [Anthropic glossary: RLHF](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/glossary)

### How models read and write

#### Token

- [ ] Approved

**Plain English.** The chunks models read and write in: a whole word, part of a word, or punctuation. Models count, limit, and charge by tokens, not words.

**Under the hood.** Text is split into tokens before the model processes it. The ratio of characters to tokens varies by model and language. API pricing is usually per million input tokens and per million output tokens.

**Customer line.** "Tokens are how AI usage gets metered, like minutes on a phone plan. Longer documents and longer answers use more tokens."

**Follow-up:** _How many tokens is a typical document?_ It varies by model and language, so the safe answer is to measure it. Most providers offer a token-counting tool, and a quick test on real documents gives you a real number for budgeting.

**Myth:** One token equals one word. **Fact:** Tokens are often pieces of words, and the ratio differs by model and language. Never quote a fixed number without checking.

Sources: [Anthropic glossary: Tokens](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/glossary)

#### Context window

- [ ] Approved

**Plain English.** How much text the model can 'see' at once: your question, the conversation so far, and any documents you give it. Think of it as the model's working memory, not its long-term knowledge.

**Under the hood.** The context window is measured in tokens and includes both input and output. Anything outside it is invisible to the model on that request. It's different from the training data, which is baked into the model's parameters.

**Customer line.** "The context window is how much the AI can read in one sitting. A bigger window means it can work across longer contracts or more call notes at once."

**Follow-up:** _So with a huge context window, will it remember our past conversations?_ Not automatically. Each request only sees what's sent in that request. 'Memory' across sessions is a product feature built on top, not part of the context window.

**Myth:** Bigger context windows mean the model remembers past conversations. **Fact:** The window only covers what's sent in the current request. Remembering across sessions requires a separate memory feature.

Sources: [Anthropic glossary: Context window](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/glossary)

### Working with models

#### Prompt

- [ ] Approved

**Plain English.** Whatever you give the model to respond to: your question, your instructions, examples, or pasted documents.

**Under the hood.** A prompt is the full input sent to the model on a request, often split into a system prompt (standing instructions) and user messages. The model's output depends heavily on how clear and specific the prompt is.

**Customer line.** "The prompt is the brief you hand the AI. A clear brief gets a better result, just like with a new hire."

**Follow-up:** _Do my users need to learn to write good prompts?_ Good products do most of the prompting behind the scenes. Users still get better results when they're specific about what they want and give context.

**Myth:** There's a secret magic phrase that unlocks better AI answers. **Fact:** What works is clarity: say what you want, give context and examples, and describe the output format.

Sources: [Anthropic: Prompt engineering overview](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/overview)

#### System prompt

- [ ] Approved

**Plain English.** Background instructions that the product gives the AI before you type anything, like a job description: who it is, what it should and shouldn't do, and how to respond.

**Under the hood.** The system prompt sits ahead of the conversation and shapes every response: role, tone, rules, available tools, and formatting. AI products put much of their behavior and guardrails here.

**Customer line.** "The system prompt is the AI's standing orders. It's one of the ways we keep it on-task and on-brand for your team."

**Follow-up:** _Can a user trick the AI into ignoring those instructions?_ It's a real risk called prompt injection. Good products don't rely on the system prompt alone. They also limit what the AI is allowed to access and do.

**Myth:** A system prompt is a security control that users can't get around. **Fact:** System prompts guide behavior but can be manipulated. Real security comes from permissions and limits outside the model.

Sources: [Anthropic: Prompt engineering overview](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/overview); [OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications](https://owasp.org/www-project-top-10-for-large-language-model-applications/)

#### Prompt engineering

- [ ] Approved

**Plain English.** The skill of writing and testing prompts to get reliable, high-quality output from a model.

**Under the hood.** It covers techniques like clear instructions, examples (few-shot), structured formatting, assigning a role, letting the model think step by step, and chaining prompts together. It works best when you have success criteria and tests to measure against.

**Customer line.** "Prompt engineering is how we tune the AI's behavior without retraining it. It's fast to change and easy to test."

**Follow-up:** _Isn't prompt engineering just a fad?_ The 'magic words' side is fading as models improve. Writing clear instructions and testing them against real examples is lasting skill.

**Myth:** Prompt engineering can fix any AI problem. **Fact:** Some issues, like speed or cost, are better solved by choosing a different model, and some need better data or retrieval.

Sources: [Anthropic: Prompt engineering overview](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/overview)

#### Few-shot prompting _(also: multishot prompting, examples in the prompt)_

- [ ] Approved

**Plain English.** Showing the model a few examples of what you want before asking it to do the task. 'Zero-shot' means no examples.

**Under the hood.** Including a few input-and-output examples in the prompt steers the format, tone, and decisions of the response without any retraining. It's one of the most reliable prompting techniques.

**Customer line.** "Instead of retraining the AI, we just show it a few good examples, the way you'd train a new rep with sample emails."

**Follow-up:** _How many examples do you need?_ Often three to five varied examples are enough. The key is that they cover the different cases you care about.

**Myth:** To teach an AI a new format, you have to retrain it. **Fact:** A handful of examples in the prompt usually does the job.

Sources: [Anthropic: Prompt engineering overview](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/overview)

#### Temperature

- [ ] Approved

**Plain English.** A setting for how creative or predictable the model's answers are. Low temperature means safer and more consistent, and high means more varied and creative.

**Under the hood.** Temperature changes how the model picks among likely next tokens. Low values favor the most probable choice, and high values let less likely choices through. Even at zero, outputs aren't guaranteed to be identical every time.

**Customer line.** "For things like summaries and data extraction we keep the AI's 'creativity dial' low so answers are consistent."

**Follow-up:** _If you set it to zero, will I always get the exact same answer?_ Usually very similar, but not guaranteed identical. That's why important outputs should be checked, not assumed.

**Myth:** Temperature zero makes the model's answers fully deterministic and correct. **Fact:** It makes outputs more consistent, not more correct, and even then they can vary slightly.

Sources: [Anthropic glossary: Temperature](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/glossary)

#### Reasoning (thinking) models _(also: extended thinking, chain of thought)_

- [ ] Approved

**Plain English.** Models that work through a problem step by step before giving their final answer, which helps on harder tasks like math, analysis, and multi-step work.

**Under the hood.** With thinking turned on, the model writes out intermediate reasoning (trying approaches, checking work) before answering. That improves quality on complex tasks, but the thinking tokens are billed as output and add time.

**Customer line.** "For hard questions the AI can 'show its work' before answering. It's slower and costs a bit more, so we use it where accuracy matters most."

**Follow-up:** _Why not turn thinking on for everything?_ It adds cost and wait time. Simple tasks like a quick summary don't benefit much.

**Myth:** Reasoning models can't make mistakes because they check their work. **Fact:** They make fewer errors on complex tasks, but their reasoning can still be wrong.

Sources: [Anthropic: Thinking](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/thinking)

### Risks and quality

#### Hallucination

- [ ] Approved

**Plain English.** When an AI states something false or made up as if it were true, like a fake statistic or a citation that doesn't exist.

**Under the hood.** Models generate likely-sounding text, not verified facts, so they can produce confident errors. You reduce it by letting the model say 'I don't know,' grounding answers in provided documents, requiring citations, and verifying claims. You can't eliminate it entirely.

**Customer line.** "No AI is hallucination-free, and anyone who says otherwise is overselling. What matters is how a product reduces it: grounding in your data, showing sources, and keeping a human on important calls."

**Follow-up:** _How do I know when it's making something up?_ Look for products that cite their sources so you can click through and check. If an answer can't point to where it came from, treat it as a draft.

**Myth:** Hallucinations only happen with cheap or old models. **Fact:** Even the best models still hallucinate sometimes. Techniques like grounding reduce it but don't eliminate it.

Sources: [Anthropic: Reduce hallucinations](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/test-and-evaluate/strengthen-guardrails/reduce-hallucinations)

#### Evals (evaluations)

- [ ] Approved

**Plain English.** Tests that measure how well an AI system performs on realistic examples, so you're judging it on evidence, not vibes.

**Under the hood.** An eval set mirrors real tasks, including edge cases, and grades the outputs by code (exact match, similarity), by a separate model acting as grader, or by people. Teams rerun evals whenever they change a prompt or model, to catch regressions.

**Customer line.** "Before we change anything in the AI, we run it against a test set of real examples, so improvements are measured, not guessed."

**Follow-up:** _Can we test it on our own data before buying?_ That's the best way to evaluate. Agree up front on a set of real examples and what 'good' looks like, then score the results.

**Myth:** Public benchmark scores tell you how a model will do on your tasks. **Fact:** Benchmarks are a rough guide. Testing on your own examples is far more predictive.

Sources: [Anthropic: Define success criteria and build evaluations](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/test-and-evaluate/develop-tests)

#### Prompt injection

- [ ] Approved

**Plain English.** An attack where someone hides instructions in text the AI reads, like an email or webpage, to trick it into ignoring its rules or leaking information.

**Under the hood.** OWASP ranks it as the top risk for LLM applications. Direct injection comes from the user's own message. Indirect injection hides in content the AI processes, like documents, websites, or tool results. It's especially risky for agents that can take actions. Defenses include limiting permissions, separating instructions from data, and requiring approval for sensitive actions.

**Customer line.** "Prompt injection is the AI version of phishing. The strongest defense isn't a clever prompt, it's limiting what the AI is allowed to access and do."

**Follow-up:** _Can it be fully prevented?_ Not completely today. That's why good products limit the damage: least-privilege access, human approval for risky actions, and monitoring.

**Myth:** Prompt injection only happens when a user types something malicious. **Fact:** The indirect kind hides in documents, emails, or web pages the AI reads, with no bad user involved.

Sources: [OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications](https://owasp.org/www-project-top-10-for-large-language-model-applications/)

### Search and retrieval

#### Embeddings

- [ ] Approved

**Plain English.** A way of turning text into a list of numbers that captures its meaning, so a computer can tell that 'car' and 'automobile' are close even though the words are different.

**Under the hood.** An embedding model converts text (or images) into a vector. Texts with similar meaning end up close together, so you can measure similarity with math. This powers semantic search, recommendations, and the retrieval step in RAG.

**Customer line.** "Embeddings let the system search by meaning, not just keywords, so asking about 'customers leaving' also finds notes that say 'churn.'"

**Follow-up:** _Is that the same as the chatbot model?_ No, it's usually a separate, smaller model built just for this. For example, Anthropic doesn't make one and points developers to providers like Voyage AI.

**Myth:** Embeddings are a compressed copy of your documents that can be read back. **Fact:** They're a numeric fingerprint of meaning, used for comparing and finding things, not for storing the full text.

Sources: [Anthropic: Embeddings](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/embeddings)

#### Vector database

- [ ] Approved

**Plain English.** A database built to store embeddings and quickly find the ones closest in meaning to a question.

**Under the hood.** Vector databases (or vector extensions like pgvector for Postgres) store vectors and run fast nearest-neighbor searches. In a RAG system, documents are embedded and stored, then the most relevant chunks are pulled back for each question.

**Customer line.** "The vector database is the library index that lets the AI find the right page of your documentation in a fraction of a second."

**Follow-up:** _Where is that database hosted, and who can access it?_ That's a fair data-security question. It contains a representation of your content, so it should get the same access controls and hosting terms as the original data.

**Myth:** You need a special standalone vector database product to do AI search. **Fact:** Many teams use a vector extension inside a database they already have, like Postgres with pgvector.

Sources: [Anthropic: Embeddings (RAG and vector search)](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/embeddings)

#### RAG (Retrieval-augmented generation) _(also: RAG, grounding)_

- [ ] Approved

**Plain English.** Before answering, the system looks up relevant information from your documents and hands it to the AI, so the answer is based on your real content instead of just the model's memory.

**Under the hood.** When a question comes in, relevant passages are retrieved (often with embeddings and a vector database) and placed into the context window alongside the question. This grounds the answer in evidence, supports citations, and keeps answers current without retraining. Quality depends on what gets retrieved.

**Customer line.** "RAG means the AI answers from your knowledge base, not from the internet at large, and it can show you which document each answer came from."

**Follow-up:** _If our documents are wrong or outdated, what happens?_ The answers will be too. RAG is only as good as the content it retrieves, so it's worth cleaning up the knowledge base before launch.

**Myth:** RAG retrains the model on your documents. **Fact:** The model doesn't change. Relevant documents are fetched and shown to it at question time.

Sources: [Anthropic glossary: RAG](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/glossary); [Anthropic: Reduce hallucinations](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/test-and-evaluate/strengthen-guardrails/reduce-hallucinations)

### Agents

#### Tool use _(also: function calling)_

- [ ] Approved

**Plain English.** Letting the AI use other software, like searching the web, checking a calendar, or looking up a record in a CRM, instead of only writing text.

**Under the hood.** The developer describes available tools to the model. The model decides when a tool would help and returns a structured request (which tool, with what inputs). The application or the provider runs the tool and sends the result back, and the model uses it to answer.

**Customer line.** "Tool use is what lets the AI actually do things in your systems, not just talk about them, and you control which tools it's allowed to touch."

**Follow-up:** _Can it take actions without asking us first?_ Only if it's set up that way. Well-built products let you require approval for actions, especially anything that changes data or contacts customers.

**Myth:** When an AI uses a tool, the model itself is running the software. **Fact:** The model only asks for the tool call. Your application or the provider's servers actually run it.

Sources: [Anthropic: Tool use overview](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/agents-and-tools/tool-use/overview)

#### AI agent

- [ ] Approved

**Plain English.** An AI that works toward a goal across multiple steps, deciding for itself which tools to use and what to do next, instead of just answering one question.

**Under the hood.** Anthropic's definition: agents are systems where the model dynamically directs its own process and tool use. Agents trade higher cost and latency for flexibility, so they suit open-ended tasks. Well-defined tasks are often better served by a simpler fixed workflow.

**Customer line.** "A chatbot answers questions. An agent takes a goal, like 'prep me for my renewal call,' and works through the steps to get it done."

**Follow-up:** _Is every AI product with 'agent' in the name actually an agent?_ No. The term gets stretched. A useful test: does the AI choose its own next steps, or is it following a fixed script someone built?

**Myth:** An agent is always better than a simple AI workflow. **Fact:** Agents are slower and cost more. For predictable tasks, a fixed workflow is often more reliable.

Sources: [Anthropic: Building effective agents](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/building-effective-agents)

#### Workflow vs agent

- [ ] Approved

**Plain English.** In a workflow, a developer decides the steps in advance and the AI fills in each one. In an agent, the AI decides the steps itself.

**Under the hood.** Anthropic describes workflows as models and tools orchestrated through predefined code paths, and agents as systems where the model directs its own process. Their advice is to start with the simplest solution and add complexity only when it clearly helps.

**Customer line.** "For repeatable tasks, we use fixed workflows because they're predictable. We save agents for messier tasks where flexibility is worth it."

**Follow-up:** _Which one is more trustworthy?_ Workflows are easier to predict and audit. Agents are more flexible but need stronger guardrails and monitoring.

**Myth:** 'Agentic' just means the product uses AI. **Fact:** It specifically means the AI chooses its own steps and actions, which is a meaningful difference for risk and reliability.

Sources: [Anthropic: Building effective agents](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/building-effective-agents)

#### MCP (Model Context Protocol) _(also: MCP)_

- [ ] Approved

**Plain English.** An open standard for plugging AI assistants into other apps and data, often described as 'USB-C for AI.' Build a connector once and any MCP-compatible AI can use it.

**Under the hood.** MCP standardizes how applications provide context and tools to models. An MCP server exposes data and actions (for example, a CRM or a file store), and MCP clients (AI apps) connect to it in a consistent way. Anthropic introduced it, and it's open for anyone to use.

**Customer line.** "MCP is a common plug format, so connecting AI to your systems doesn't need a custom integration for every tool."

**Follow-up:** _Does MCP make it secure by default?_ No. It standardizes the connection. You still need to control which servers you trust and what each one is allowed to access.

**Myth:** MCP only works with Claude. **Fact:** It's an open protocol meant to work across AI apps and models.

Sources: [Anthropic glossary: MCP](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/glossary); [Model Context Protocol docs](https://modelcontextprotocol.io/)

### Performance and cost

#### Latency (and time to first token) _(also: TTFT)_

- [ ] Approved

**Plain English.** How long you wait for the AI to respond. Time to first token (TTFT) is how fast the first words appear, which is what makes it feel quick.

**Under the hood.** Latency depends on model size, prompt length, output length, server load, and network. Streaming shows tokens as they're produced, so a low TTFT feels responsive even if the full answer takes longer.

**Customer line.** "Bigger models are usually smarter but slower. For live use like a support chat, speed can matter as much as raw intelligence."

**Follow-up:** _Why is it fast sometimes and slow other times?_ Longer inputs, longer answers, and busy periods on the provider's side all add time. Products often pick a faster model for real-time features.

**Myth:** A slow AI response means the answer will be better. **Fact:** Speed mostly reflects model size, workload, and length, not quality.

Sources: [Anthropic glossary: Latency and TTFT](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/glossary)

## AI in SaaS sales and CS (20)

### Data and security

#### "Do you train on our data?"

- [ ] Approved

**Plain English.** Usually the first AI question security and legal teams ask: will our information be used to improve the vendor's model, or the model provider's?

**Under the hood.** There are two layers to check: the product vendor's policy and the policy of the model provider underneath it (for example, Anthropic, OpenAI, or a cloud platform). Business and API terms commonly say customer data isn't used for training without permission, but that's a contract term, not a law of physics. Get it in writing.

**Customer line.** "Fair question, and there are two layers to it: our policy and our model provider's. I'll send you the exact contract language for both rather than paraphrase it."

**Follow-up:** _What about the AI vendor you use under the hood?_ Name the provider, point to their commercial data terms, and confirm whether your company has a data processing agreement (DPA) with them. If you don't know, say you'll confirm. Guessing here kills trust.

**Myth:** If a company uses AI, it must be training on your data. **Fact:** Using a model (inference) is separate from training it. Many business agreements explicitly rule out training on customer data.

Sources: [Anthropic: API and data retention](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/manage-claude/api-and-data-retention)

#### Data retention and zero data retention (ZDR) _(also: ZDR)_

- [ ] Approved

**Plain English.** How long prompts and AI responses are stored after a request, and by whom. Zero data retention means they aren't kept after the response, apart from limited exceptions.

**Under the hood.** Retention can apply at the product layer (chat history, logs) and the model-provider layer. Providers like Anthropic offer ZDR arrangements for eligible features. Some features, like stored files or batch jobs, need storage by design, so eligibility varies feature by feature. Regulated data such as health information needs its own arrangements, like a HIPAA business associate agreement (BAA).

**Customer line.** "Retention is set feature by feature, so let's map which features your team will actually use and confirm the retention terms for each one."

**Follow-up:** _Can we get zero retention on everything?_ Often not every feature, because some, like saved history, need storage to work. The honest answer is a feature list showing what's covered.

**Myth:** Zero data retention means nothing is ever logged anywhere. **Fact:** It covers specific data for specific features. Account info, billing, and some safety records may still be kept. Read the scope.

Sources: [Anthropic: API and data retention](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/manage-claude/api-and-data-retention)

#### Data residency

- [ ] Approved

**Plain English.** Where in the world your data is stored and processed, such as 'EU only' or 'US only.' It often comes up with European, government, or regulated customers.

**Under the hood.** With AI, check two things: where data is stored (databases, logs) and where inference runs (which region's servers the model runs on). Cloud platforms often let you pin a region, and some model providers offer regional processing options. Requirements usually come from regulations like GDPR or from customer contracts.

**Customer line.** "With AI there are two residency questions: where your data sits, and where the model runs when it processes it. Let's confirm both for your region."

**Follow-up:** _Does the AI processing stay in the EU too?_ That's the right question, because storage and processing can be in different places. Confirm with your solutions or security team before answering.

**Myth:** If the data is stored in the EU, all AI processing happens in the EU. **Fact:** Storage and model processing can be in different regions unless the setup pins both.

Sources: [Anthropic: API and data retention](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/manage-claude/api-and-data-retention)

#### PII and sensitive data _(also: PII, PHI)_

- [ ] Approved

**Plain English.** PII is personally identifiable information: anything that can identify a person, like names, emails, phone numbers, or ID numbers. PHI is protected health information.

**Under the hood.** AI features often process PII without anyone noticing (call transcripts, tickets, emails). Common controls include redacting or masking data before it reaches the model, role-based access, retention limits, and contracts like DPAs (or BAAs for health data in the US).

**Customer line.** "Let's map where personal data shows up in the workflow. Then we can show you exactly how it's handled at each step, including what the model sees and what gets redacted."

**Follow-up:** _Can you strip out personal data before the AI sees it?_ Many products offer redaction or masking. Ask whether it's on by default and how accurate it is on your kind of data.

**Myth:** If you don't upload a customer list, the AI never touches PII. **Fact:** Call notes, emails, and support tickets are full of names and contact details.

Sources: [Anthropic: API and data retention (HIPAA readiness)](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/manage-claude/api-and-data-retention)

#### Guardrails

- [ ] Approved

**Plain English.** Controls that keep an AI system on track and safe: what it can talk about, what data it can see, what actions it can take, and what gets blocked or flagged.

**Under the hood.** Guardrails include system prompt rules, input and output filters, permission limits on tools and data, requiring citations, human approval steps, rate limits, and monitoring. The strongest ones work outside the model (like permissions), since instructions alone can be bypassed.

**Customer line.** "Guardrails are the bumpers on the bowling lane. We set what the AI can see and do, and the riskiest actions always need a human."

**Follow-up:** _Can admins customize the guardrails?_ Ask your product team what's configurable: data access, allowed actions, blocked topics, and approval rules. That's a strong selling point when it exists.

**Myth:** Telling the AI 'never do X' in its instructions is a reliable guardrail. **Fact:** Instructions help but can be bypassed. Hard limits on access and permissions are more reliable.

Sources: [Anthropic: Reduce hallucinations (Strengthen guardrails)](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/test-and-evaluate/strengthen-guardrails/reduce-hallucinations); [OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications](https://owasp.org/www-project-top-10-for-large-language-model-applications/)

### Compliance and governance

#### SOC 2 (and what it means for AI) _(also: SOC 2 Type II)_

- [ ] Approved

**Plain English.** SOC 2 is an independent audit report showing a company's security controls actually work. It's a standard ask in SaaS security reviews.

**Under the hood.** SOC 2 is based on the AICPA Trust Services Criteria: security (always included), plus optional availability, processing integrity, confidentiality, and privacy. A Type II report tests the controls over a period of time. SOC 2 wasn't designed specifically for AI, so buyers now often add AI-specific questions or ask about frameworks like ISO 42001.

**Customer line.** "Our SOC 2 covers our security controls. For the AI side, here's how we handle model providers, data flows, and testing, and I'm happy to fill out your AI questionnaire."

**Follow-up:** _Does your SOC 2 cover your AI model provider too?_ Usually not directly. Third-party providers are typically handled as subprocessors with their own reports. Offer to share the provider's compliance documents.

**Myth:** A SOC 2 report proves a company's AI is safe and accurate. **Fact:** SOC 2 tests security and operational controls, not whether AI outputs are accurate or fair.

Sources: [AICPA: SOC 2 reporting guide (Trust Services Criteria)](https://www.aicpa-cima.com/cpe-learning/publication/soc-2-reporting-on-an-examination-of-controls-at-a-service-organization-relevant-to-security-availability-processing-integrity-confidentiality-or-privacy)

#### AI governance

- [ ] Approved

**Plain English.** The rules, roles, and oversight a company puts around how it uses AI: who approves new tools, what's allowed, and how risks get tracked.

**Under the hood.** Governance usually includes an AI use policy, an inventory of AI systems, risk reviews, vendor assessments, monitoring, and clear accountability. Frameworks like NIST AI RMF and ISO 42001, and laws like the EU AI Act, give it structure.

**Customer line.** "Many of our customers have an AI governance committee. We can give you a standard pack (data flows, model provider, testing, controls) so your review moves faster."

**Follow-up:** _Who on our side should be in this conversation?_ Typically security, legal or privacy, and whoever owns AI governance or IT risk. Getting them in early prevents a late-stage stall.

**Myth:** AI governance is only for companies building their own AI models. **Fact:** Companies buying AI tools need governance too, often more, since most of their AI use comes through vendors.

Sources: [NIST AI Risk Management Framework](https://www.nist.gov/itl/ai-risk-management-framework)

#### Responsible AI

- [ ] Approved

**Plain English.** Building and using AI in ways that are fair, safe, private, transparent, and accountable.

**Under the hood.** Microsoft's six responsible AI principles (part of the AI-900 material) are fairness, reliability and safety, privacy and security, inclusiveness, transparency, and accountability. Other organizations use similar lists. Anthropic's 'helpful, honest, harmless' (HHH) is a related research framing.

**Customer line.** "For us, responsible AI shows up in specific features: citing sources, human review for high-stakes actions, admin controls, and clear data terms."

**Follow-up:** _How do you test for bias?_ Have your product team's real answer ready. If there's no formal process, don't invent one. Say what exists and follow up.

**Myth:** Responsible AI is just a marketing page. **Fact:** Buyers increasingly ask for evidence: testing, controls, and documentation. Being able to show it wins deals.

Sources: [Microsoft Learn: Identify guiding principles for responsible AI](https://learn.microsoft.com/training/modules/embrace-responsible-ai-principles-practices/3-identify-guiding-principles-responsible-ai); [Anthropic glossary: HHH](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/glossary)

#### NIST AI Risk Management Framework _(also: AI RMF)_

- [ ] Approved

**Plain English.** A free, voluntary US framework from NIST that helps organizations spot and manage the risks of AI. It's widely used as a governance checklist.

**Under the hood.** AI RMF 1.0 was released in January 2023 and has four functions: Govern (policies and accountability), Map (understand the context and risks), Measure (test and monitor), and Manage (prioritize and respond). NIST later added a Generative AI Profile with risks specific to generative AI.

**Customer line.** "If your team uses NIST AI RMF, we can map our controls to its four functions (Govern, Map, Measure, Manage) to make your review easier."

**Follow-up:** _Is it a certification?_ No, it's voluntary guidance, not a certificate you can earn. ISO 42001 is the certifiable standard.

**Myth:** The NIST AI RMF is a law companies must follow. **Fact:** It's voluntary guidance, though contracts and some sectors may expect alignment with it.

Sources: [NIST AI Risk Management Framework](https://www.nist.gov/itl/ai-risk-management-framework)

#### EU AI Act

- [ ] Approved

**Plain English.** The European Union's AI law. It sorts AI uses by risk level and puts stricter rules on riskier ones.

**Under the hood.** The Act entered into force in August 2024, with obligations phasing in over several years. It defines four tiers: unacceptable risk (banned, like social scoring), high risk (strict requirements, like AI used in hiring or critical infrastructure), limited risk (transparency duties, like telling people they're talking to a chatbot), and minimal risk (most AI, few added duties). Deadlines have been revised, so check current dates before quoting them.

**Customer line.** "Most business productivity AI falls in the lower-risk tiers, but uses like hiring decisions can be high-risk. It's worth checking where your planned use fits."

**Follow-up:** _Does it apply to us if we're a US company?_ It can, if you offer AI systems in the EU or their outputs are used there. That's a question for their legal team, but it's smart to flag it.

**Myth:** The EU AI Act bans generative AI. **Fact:** It bans a short list of specific practices and regulates others by risk level. Most generative AI use is allowed.

Sources: [EUR-Lex: Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 (AI Act)](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj)

#### ISO/IEC 42001

- [ ] Approved

**Plain English.** An international standard for running an AI management system, which a company can get certified against. Think of it as ISO 27001, but for AI.

**Under the hood.** ISO/IEC 42001:2023, published in December 2023, sets requirements for establishing, running, and improving an AI management system. It uses the same structure as ISO 27001 and 9001, which makes it easier to add for companies that already hold those certifications.

**Customer line.** "If your procurement team asks for AI-specific certification, ISO 42001 is the one to check for, since SOC 2 doesn't cover AI governance directly."

**Follow-up:** _Do you have it?_ Know your company's real status: certified, in progress, or not planned. If you're not certified, explain which existing controls cover similar ground.

**Myth:** ISO 42001 certifies that an AI model is accurate. **Fact:** It certifies the management processes around AI, not the outputs of any specific model.

Sources: [ISO: ISO/IEC 42001:2023 AI management systems](https://www.iso.org/standard/42001)

### Selling AI

#### Human in the loop _(also: HITL)_

- [ ] Approved

**Plain English.** Designing an AI process so a person reviews or approves the AI's work before it counts, especially for important decisions or actions.

**Under the hood.** Setups range from review before anything is sent, to approval only for risky actions, to spot-checking samples after the fact. The right level depends on the cost of a mistake. It's also a key control for agents that can take actions.

**Customer line.** "The AI drafts, your team decides. You choose where a human has to sign off, and that's usually anything customer-facing or irreversible."

**Follow-up:** _Doesn't human review cancel out the time savings?_ Reviewing a good draft is much faster than starting from scratch, and you can loosen review as trust builds on low-risk tasks.

**Myth:** Human in the loop means the AI isn't good enough to use. **Fact:** It's a standard safety design, the same way important documents get a second set of eyes.

Sources: [Anthropic: Building effective agents](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/building-effective-agents); [NIST AI Risk Management Framework](https://www.nist.gov/itl/ai-risk-management-framework)

#### Copilot vs agent

- [ ] Approved

**Plain English.** A copilot helps a person do their work: it suggests, drafts, and summarizes, while the person stays in control. An agent works on a task more independently and takes actions on its own.

**Under the hood.** Copilots are usually built into an app the user is already in, like Microsoft 365 Copilot. Agents chain multiple steps and tool calls with less human input. Many products sit on a spectrum, with agents asking for approval at key points.

**Customer line.** "Copilots make your people faster. Agents take whole tasks off their plate. Most teams start with copilots and move to agents as trust builds."

**Follow-up:** _Which should we start with?_ Usually a copilot on a high-volume task, because it's lower risk and easy to measure. Then add agent features where the process is well understood.

**Myth:** 'Copilot' is just Microsoft's product name. **Fact:** Microsoft uses it as a brand, but it's also a general term for assistive AI built into your workflow.

Sources: [Microsoft Learn: Introduction to generative AI and agents](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/training/modules/fundamentals-generative-ai/); [Anthropic: Building effective agents](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/building-effective-agents)

#### Build vs buy (for AI)

- [ ] Approved

**Plain English.** Should the customer build their own AI tool on a model API, or buy a product that already does the job? Expect this objection now that model APIs are easy to access.

**Under the hood.** Building gives control and customization, but the team then owns integrations, security reviews, evals, model upgrades, monitoring, and support forever. Buying trades some control for speed and someone else maintaining all of it. A common middle path is to buy the platform and customize it.

**Customer line.** "The demo is the easy part to build. The hard part is the next three years: integrations, security, model upgrades, and support. That's what you're really buying."

**Follow-up:** _Our engineers say they can build this in a month._ They probably can build a prototype. Ask what it costs to run, secure, and improve over time, and what those engineers won't be building instead.

**Myth:** Building on a model API is always cheaper than buying. **Fact:** Upfront it can be. Total cost includes engineering time, maintenance, compliance, and opportunity cost.

Sources: [DeepLearning.AI: Generative AI for Everyone (Generative AI projects)](https://www.deeplearning.ai/courses/generative-ai-for-everyone)

#### Pilot to production _(also: POC, proof of concept)_

- [ ] Approved

**Plain English.** Moving from a small AI test with a few users to full rollout. Many AI projects get stuck in endless pilots.

**Under the hood.** Pilots stall when there are no success criteria, no executive owner, unresolved security concerns, or messy data. A pilot is more likely to convert when it has defined metrics, a time limit, real users on real work, early security review, and a rollout plan agreed in advance.

**Customer line.** "Before we start the pilot, let's agree on what success looks like and what happens if we hit it. That way the pilot leads to a decision, not another pilot."

**Follow-up:** _Can we just try it for a few months and see?_ You can, but open-ended pilots rarely convert. A 30 to 60 day pilot with clear metrics gives you a real answer faster.

**Myth:** A great demo means the rollout will go smoothly. **Fact:** Real data, real users, and security reviews are where most AI projects slow down.

Sources: [DeepLearning.AI: Generative AI for Everyone (Generative AI projects)](https://www.deeplearning.ai/courses/generative-ai-for-everyone); [Anthropic: Define success criteria and build evaluations](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/test-and-evaluate/develop-tests)

#### Model choice and vendor lock-in _(also: model-agnostic)_

- [ ] Approved

**Plain English.** Which AI model a product uses, and whether it can switch to another one. 'Model-agnostic' products can swap models as better or cheaper ones come out.

**Under the hood.** Models improve and change price quickly, so tying a product to one model is a risk. Products often route tasks to different models (fast and cheap for simple tasks, most capable for hard ones). Buyers also care about where the model is hosted, for example through their existing cloud provider.

**Customer line.** "We pick the right model for each task and can switch as the market moves, so you get the improvements without migrating anything."

**Follow-up:** _Can we bring our own model or use our cloud provider's?_ Some products support this and some don't. Check with your product team, because it's a common enterprise requirement.

**Myth:** The product that uses the newest, biggest model is always the best. **Fact:** Fit matters more: accuracy on your task, speed, cost, and data terms.

Sources: [Anthropic: Prompt engineering overview (choosing a model)](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/overview)

#### AI washing

- [ ] Approved

**Plain English.** Overstating how much AI a product uses, or what it can do, for marketing. Buyers are increasingly skeptical because of it.

**Under the hood.** Regulators have acted on misleading AI claims using existing rules: in March 2024 the SEC settled charges against two investment advisers (Delphia and Global Predictions) for overstating their AI use, and the FTC launched an enforcement sweep called Operation AI Comply in September 2024. Buyers respond by asking for demos on their own data, evals, and specifics about what's actually AI and what's rules or human work.

**Customer line.** "I'd rather show you than tell you. Let's run it on your real examples and you can judge what the AI is actually doing."

**Follow-up:** _How much of your product is actually AI?_ Be specific: which features use a model, which use rules, and where people are involved. Precise answers build credibility fast.

**Myth:** Saying 'AI-powered' in every pitch helps you win more deals. **Fact:** Vague claims now raise skepticism. Specific, provable claims win.

Sources: [SEC press release 2024-36 (AI washing charges)](https://www.sec.gov/newsroom/press-releases/2024-36); [DeepLearning.AI: Generative AI for Everyone (what generative AI can and cannot do)](https://www.deeplearning.ai/courses/generative-ai-for-everyone)

### Pricing and value

#### Usage-based (token) pricing vs seat pricing

- [ ] Approved

**Plain English.** AI model providers usually charge by usage (tokens in and tokens out). SaaS products have traditionally charged per user (seat). Many AI products now mix the two.

**Under the hood.** Model APIs price input and output tokens separately, and output usually costs more. Costs can be lowered with prompt caching (reusing repeated input at a discount), batch processing (cheaper, not real-time), and choosing smaller models where they're good enough. Vendors may pass this through as credits, usage tiers, or caps on top of seats.

**Customer line.** "Seats are predictable. Usage scales with value. We can structure it so you have a predictable budget with room to grow."

**Follow-up:** _How do we avoid a surprise bill?_ Ask about spending caps, usage alerts, and admin dashboards. A good vendor will help estimate usage from a pilot.

**Myth:** AI usage costs are basically zero once you've paid for the license. **Fact:** Every AI request has a real computing cost, which is why AI features often come with usage limits or credits.

Sources: [Anthropic: Prompt caching](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-caching); [Anthropic: Batch processing](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/batch-processing)

#### AI ROI

- [ ] Approved

**Plain English.** Showing that the AI is worth what it costs, in time saved, revenue gained, risk reduced, or quality improved.

**Under the hood.** Strong AI ROI cases measure a baseline first (time per task, error rate, volume), run a pilot on real work, and compare. Time saved only counts if it goes somewhere useful. Adoption rate is often the deciding factor, since unused seats deliver nothing.

**Customer line.** "Let's pick one workflow, measure how long it takes today, and measure it again after 30 days. Then the ROI is your number, not my slide."

**Follow-up:** _Vendors always promise huge productivity gains. Why should I believe yours?_ Don't take anyone's word for it, including mine. Measure it in a pilot with a baseline you agree on up front.

**Myth:** Hours saved automatically equals money saved. **Fact:** Only if the time is redirected to valuable work or reduces costs. Otherwise it's a soft benefit.

Sources: [DeepLearning.AI: Generative AI for Everyone (Generative AI in business)](https://www.deeplearning.ai/courses/generative-ai-for-everyone)

#### False positives and false negatives _(also: precision and recall)_

- [ ] Approved

**Plain English.** A false positive is when the AI flags something that turns out to be nothing, like a real email sent to spam. A false negative is something real it missed, like a spam email that lands in your inbox. Every AI system that flags, filters, or scores things trades one against the other.

**Under the hood.** Precision is the share of flagged items that are truly relevant (few false positives). Recall is the share of truly relevant items that got flagged (few false negatives). Tuning a threshold usually improves one at the expense of the other. The right balance depends on which mistake costs more.

**Customer line.** "No AI gets every call right, so the real question is which mistake costs you more: flagging too much, or missing something important. We can tune it toward whichever one matters more to your team."

**Follow-up:** _Can't you just get both to zero?_ No real system does. Anyone claiming zero false positives and zero misses is overselling. What matters is a balance that fits the cost of each mistake.

**Myth:** A higher accuracy percentage always means a better system. **Fact:** If real events are rare, a system can be 99% 'accurate' while missing most of them. Precision and recall tell you more.

Sources: [Anthropic: Define success criteria and build evaluations](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/test-and-evaluate/develop-tests)
