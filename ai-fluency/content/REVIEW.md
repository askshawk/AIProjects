## AI fundamentals (90)

### Core concepts

#### Artificial intelligence _(also: AI)_

- [ ] Approved

**In a few words.** Machines doing tasks that normally need human judgment

**Plain English.** The broad goal of getting computers to do things that usually take human judgment: understanding language, recognizing images, making predictions, or writing text.

**Under the hood.** AI is an umbrella term. Machine learning is the main way modern AI is built, deep learning is a powerful kind of machine learning, and generative AI and LLMs are the newest, most visible branch. Older AI also included hand-written rule systems.

**Customer line.** "AI is the umbrella. When people say AI today, they usually mean generative AI: tools that write, summarize, and answer questions."

**Follow-up:** _So is every AI product the same kind of AI?_ No. Some predict or classify (like fraud scores), some generate content (like drafting emails). Asking which kind a product uses tells you a lot about what it can and can't do.

**Myth:** AI and generative AI mean the same thing. **Fact:** Generative AI is one branch of AI. Plenty of AI, like spam filters and recommendations, doesn't generate anything.

Sources: [Microsoft Learn: Introduction to generative AI and agents](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/training/modules/fundamentals-generative-ai/); [DeepLearning.AI: AI for Everyone](https://www.deeplearning.ai/courses/ai-for-everyone)

#### Deep learning

- [ ] Approved

**In a few words.** Machine learning with many-layered neural networks

**Plain English.** A kind of machine learning that uses large neural networks with many layers. It's what made modern speech recognition, image recognition, and chatbots possible.

**Under the hood.** Each layer of a deep neural network transforms its input a little, and stacking many layers lets the network learn complex patterns directly from raw data like text or pixels. It needs lots of data and computing power, which is why GPUs matter so much.

**Customer line.** "Deep learning is the technique behind almost every recent AI breakthrough, including the language models in today's AI tools."

**Follow-up:** _Is deep learning different from machine learning?_ It's a subset. All deep learning is machine learning, but not all machine learning is deep. Simpler methods still work well for many prediction tasks.

**Myth:** Deep learning means the AI understands things deeply. **Fact:** The 'deep' refers to the number of layers in the network, not depth of understanding.

Sources: [Google Machine Learning Glossary](https://developers.google.com/machine-learning/glossary)

#### Neural network

- [ ] Approved

**In a few words.** Layers of simple math units that learn patterns together

**Plain English.** A model made of many simple connected units, loosely inspired by brain cells. Each unit does a tiny calculation, and together they learn to recognize patterns.

**Under the hood.** Inputs flow through layers of units; each connection has a weight (a parameter). Training adjusts the weights so the network's outputs match the examples. LLMs are very large neural networks with a specific design called a transformer.

**Customer line.** "A neural network is the structure inside the model: billions of small adjustable connections that together learned the patterns in its training data."

**Follow-up:** _Does it actually work like a brain?_ Only loosely. The name comes from a rough analogy. In practice it's large-scale math, not a copy of how brains work.

**Myth:** Neural networks are digital copies of the human brain. **Fact:** They were loosely inspired by neurons, but they work very differently from real brains.

Sources: [Google Machine Learning Glossary](https://developers.google.com/machine-learning/glossary)

#### Transformer

- [ ] Approved

**In a few words.** The neural network design behind modern language models

**Plain English.** The design (architecture) that almost every modern language model is built on. It's very good at understanding how words in a long passage relate to each other.

**Under the hood.** Introduced in the 2017 paper 'Attention Is All You Need', the transformer uses attention to weigh how relevant every token is to every other token, and it processes text in parallel, which made training on huge datasets practical. The 'T' in GPT stands for transformer.

**Customer line.** "The transformer is the blueprint modern AI models are built on. It's why they can follow long, complicated documents."

**Follow-up:** _Do I need to understand transformers to use AI?_ No. It's useful background, like knowing an engine has pistons. What matters day to day is what the model can do and how it's controlled.

**Myth:** The transformer is a specific product from one company. **Fact:** It's a published design that Google researchers introduced and that nearly every AI lab now uses.

Sources: [Vaswani et al., Attention Is All You Need (2017)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.03762)

#### Attention _(also: self-attention)_

- [ ] Approved

**In a few words.** How a model decides which words matter most to each other

**Plain English.** The mechanism that lets a model figure out which other words in a passage matter for understanding each word, like knowing 'it' refers to 'the contract' three sentences back.

**Under the hood.** For every token, attention computes how strongly it should draw on every other token in the context, then blends that information together. It's the core of the transformer, and its cost grows with context length, one reason long contexts cost more.

**Customer line.** "Attention is how the model keeps track of what refers to what across a long document."

**Follow-up:** _Is that why longer documents cost more?_ Partly. More tokens means more work for attention and more tokens billed.

**Myth:** Attention means the model is paying attention like a person does. **Fact:** It's a math operation that weighs relationships between tokens, not awareness.

Sources: [Vaswani et al., Attention Is All You Need (2017)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.03762)

#### Frontier model

- [ ] Approved

**In a few words.** The most capable models available at any given time

**Plain English.** The most advanced AI models at the cutting edge, usually from a handful of large labs. The label shifts as newer models come out.

**Under the hood.** Frontier models are the largest, most capable general-purpose models, trained with enormous compute. They lead on hard tasks like complex reasoning and coding, cost more per token, and attract the most safety and regulatory attention.

**Customer line.** "Frontier models are the most capable ones on the market. You don't always need one; smaller models are cheaper and faster for simple tasks."

**Follow-up:** _Should we always use the frontier model?_ Not for everything. Use it where quality matters most, and cheaper models for simple, high-volume work.

**Myth:** The frontier model is always the right choice. **Fact:** It's often overkill. Matching the model to the task saves cost and time.

Sources: [Anthropic: Models overview](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/models/overview)

#### Small language model _(also: SLM)_

- [ ] Approved

**In a few words.** A smaller, cheaper, faster model for simpler tasks

**Plain English.** A language model with far fewer parameters than the biggest ones. It's less capable on hard problems but much cheaper and faster, and some can run on a laptop or phone.

**Under the hood.** Small models are often made by distilling a larger model or training on carefully chosen data. They're well suited to classification, extraction, routing, and simple drafting, and they're common in on-device and high-volume uses.

#### AGI (Artificial general intelligence) _(also: AGI)_

- [ ] Approved

> Needs a better source: the cited page is a general course, not a direct definition of this term.

**In a few words.** Hypothetical AI matching humans across most tasks

**Plain English.** The idea of AI that can do most intellectual tasks as well as a person, across many areas instead of just one. There's no agreed definition or agreed timeline.

**Under the hood.** Labs and researchers define AGI differently, from 'matches humans at most economically valuable work' to broader notions of general reasoning. Today's systems are very capable in many areas but still fail in ways people wouldn't, so whether and when AGI arrives is debated.

**Customer line.** "AGI is a big-picture debate. For your rollout, what matters is what today's models can reliably do on your tasks, which we can test."

**Follow-up:** _Is your product AGI?_ No product today is AGI by any widely accepted definition, and a vendor claiming otherwise is overselling.

**Myth:** AGI has a clear, agreed definition. **Fact:** Definitions vary widely, which is why claims about it are hard to evaluate.

Sources: [DeepLearning.AI: Generative AI for Everyone](https://www.deeplearning.ai/courses/generative-ai-for-everyone)

#### Knowledge cutoff _(also: training cutoff)_

- [ ] Approved

**In a few words.** The date after which a model knows nothing on its own

**Plain English.** The point in time when a model's training data ends. It doesn't know about anything that happened after that unless the product gives it current information.

**Under the hood.** Each model has a training data cutoff. To answer questions about recent events or your latest documents, the system needs search, tool use, or retrieval (RAG) to bring fresh information into the context window.

**Customer line.** "The model's built-in knowledge stops at a date. That's why we connect it to your live data instead of relying on what it memorized."

**Follow-up:** _So it might give outdated answers?_ It can, if it's answering from memory alone. Grounding answers in your current content avoids that.

**Myth:** AI models automatically know today's news. **Fact:** Only if the product connects them to search or live data.

Sources: [Anthropic: Models overview](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/models/overview)

#### Machine learning _(also: ML)_

- [ ] Approved

> Needs a better source: the cited page is a general course, not a direct definition of this term.

**In a few words.** Software that learns patterns from examples instead of hand-written rules

**Plain English.** Instead of writing rules by hand, you show a computer lots of examples and it learns the pattern itself. A spam filter learns what spam looks like from millions of emails people marked as spam.

**Under the hood.** A model is a math function with adjustable settings. Training adjusts those settings until the model's outputs match the examples well. Supervised learning uses labeled examples, unsupervised learning finds structure in unlabeled data, and reinforcement learning learns from rewards.

**Customer line.** "Machine learning means the system learned from examples rather than following rules someone wrote, so it can handle cases nobody spelled out in advance."

**Follow-up:** _So is ML the same thing as AI?_ ML is the main way modern AI gets built, but AI is the broader goal of machines doing tasks that normally need human judgment. Today, almost every AI product you hear about is built with machine learning.

**Myth:** Machine learning systems keep learning on their own every time you use them. **Fact:** Most deployed models are frozen after training. They only get better when the vendor retrains or updates them.

Sources: [Microsoft Learn: Introduction to generative AI and agents](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/training/modules/fundamentals-generative-ai/); [DeepLearning.AI: Generative AI for Everyone](https://www.deeplearning.ai/courses/generative-ai-for-everyone)

#### Generative AI _(also: GenAI)_

- [ ] Approved

**In a few words.** AI that creates new text, images, or code

**Plain English.** AI that creates new content (text, images, code, audio) instead of only sorting or scoring existing content. ChatGPT and Claude are generative AI.

**Under the hood.** Generative models learn the patterns in their training data well enough to produce new examples that fit those patterns. Traditional or predictive AI classifies or forecasts (spam or not spam, likely to churn or not), while generative AI produces an output like a paragraph or an image.

**Customer line.** "Traditional AI answers 'which bucket does this go in?' Generative AI answers 'write me the first draft.'"

**Follow-up:** _Is generative AI accurate enough to trust?_ It's great at drafts, summaries, and first passes, but it can state wrong things confidently. The best setups keep a person reviewing anything important and connect the model to trusted data.

**Myth:** Generative AI looks up answers in a database, like a search engine. **Fact:** By default it generates text from patterns it learned in training. It only 'looks things up' if the product connects it to a search tool or your data.

Sources: [Microsoft Learn: Introduction to generative AI and agents](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/training/modules/fundamentals-generative-ai/); [DeepLearning.AI: Generative AI for Everyone](https://www.deeplearning.ai/courses/generative-ai-for-everyone)

#### Large language model _(also: LLM)_

- [ ] Approved

**In a few words.** A huge text-trained model that writes and follows instructions

**Plain English.** The engine behind chat assistants like Claude and ChatGPT. It's a huge model trained on a massive amount of text, so it can write, summarize, answer questions, and follow instructions.

**Under the hood.** An LLM is a neural network with billions of parameters, pretrained to predict the next token in text and then fine-tuned (often with RLHF) to follow instructions and act like a helpful assistant.

**Customer line.** "An LLM is the language engine. Our product is the car built around it: your data, your workflows, and the guardrails."

**Follow-up:** _Which LLM do you use?_ Be ready with the real answer from your product team. It's also worth explaining why that model was chosen (quality, cost, speed, data terms) and whether the product can switch models later.

**Myth:** An LLM understands language the way a person does. **Fact:** It's extremely good at predicting useful text from patterns. Whether that counts as 'understanding' is debated, which is why it can sound sure while being wrong.

Sources: [Anthropic glossary: LLM](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/glossary)

#### Foundation model

- [ ] Approved

> Needs a better source: the cited page is a general course, not a direct definition of this term.

**In a few words.** A big general model that many products build on

**Plain English.** A big general-purpose model that lots of different products are built on top of, like a base layer. Claude, GPT, and Gemini are foundation models.

**Under the hood.** Foundation models are trained once at enormous cost on broad data, then adapted for many tasks through prompting, retrieval, or fine-tuning. Most AI startups don't train their own. They build on a foundation model from a provider like Anthropic, OpenAI, or Google.

**Customer line.** "Like most AI products, we build on a foundation model and add what makes it useful for your team: your data, the workflow, and the controls."

**Follow-up:** _If you're built on someone else's model, what stops me from just using that model directly?_ Nothing stops you from trying. The value is in everything around the model: the integrations, the security and admin controls, the workflow, and the support. That's the honest build vs buy conversation.

**Myth:** Every AI company trains its own model from scratch. **Fact:** Very few do. Most build products on top of models from a handful of providers.

Sources: [Microsoft Learn: Introduction to generative AI and agents](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/training/modules/fundamentals-generative-ai/)

#### Parameters _(also: weights)_

- [ ] Approved

**In a few words.** The internal settings a model tunes while learning

**Plain English.** The millions or billions of internal settings a model adjusts while it learns. They're where the model's 'knowledge' is stored.

**Under the hood.** Parameters (also called weights) are the numbers inside a neural network that training tunes. Parameter count is a rough measure of model size. More parameters can mean more capability, but also higher cost and slower responses.

**Customer line.** "Parameters are the model's internal dials. Bigger isn't automatically better for you. The right model is the one that's accurate enough at a cost and speed that fit the job."

**Follow-up:** _Should I pick the vendor with the biggest model?_ Not by size alone. Ask how it performs on tasks like yours, how fast it responds, and what it costs at your volume.

**Myth:** A model with more parameters is always smarter. **Fact:** Training data, training methods, and fine-tuning matter a lot. Smaller newer models often beat larger older ones.

Sources: [Anthropic glossary: LLM](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/glossary)

#### Training vs inference

- [ ] Approved

**In a few words.** Learning happens once; inference is each time you use it

**Plain English.** Training is when the model learns, which happens once, up front, and is expensive. Inference is when you use it: every time you ask a question and get an answer.

**Under the hood.** Training adjusts the model's parameters using large datasets and lots of computing power. Inference runs the finished, frozen model on new input to produce an output. Usage-based AI pricing is mostly paying for inference.

**Customer line.** "Training is building the brain. Inference is the brain answering your question. When you use the product, you're doing inference, and your prompts don't retrain the model."

**Follow-up:** _So does my data end up training the model when I use it?_ Using the model (inference) doesn't change it by itself. Whether the vendor later uses your data for training is a policy question, so check the contract and data terms.

**Myth:** Every question you ask an AI immediately teaches it something new. **Fact:** Inference doesn't change the model. Any learning from your data would happen only if the vendor chooses to retrain on it later.

Sources: [Anthropic glossary: Pretraining](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/glossary); [Microsoft Learn: Introduction to generative AI and agents](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/training/modules/fundamentals-generative-ai/)

#### Multimodal

- [ ] Approved

> Needs a better source: the cited page is a general course, not a direct definition of this term.

**In a few words.** AI that handles images, audio, or files as well as text

**Plain English.** AI that works with more than one kind of input or output: text plus images, audio, video, or documents like PDFs.

**Under the hood.** Multimodal models process several data types in one model. For example, they can read a screenshot or chart and answer questions about it. Support varies: a model might read images but not create them.

**Customer line.** "Multimodal means you can hand the AI a screenshot, a PDF, or a photo, not just typed text."

**Follow-up:** _Can it read our scanned documents and charts?_ Often yes, but accuracy on messy scans and complex charts varies. It's worth testing with a few of your real documents.

#### Open-weight vs closed models _(also: open source models)_

- [ ] Approved

> Needs a better source: the cited page is a general course, not a direct definition of this term.

**In a few words.** Models you can download and run yourself

**Plain English.** Open-weight models can be downloaded and run on your own servers. Closed models, like Claude or GPT, are used only through the provider's service.

**Under the hood.** With open weights (for example, Llama or Mistral models), the trained parameters are published, so you can self-host and customize them, but you own the hosting, scaling, and safety work. Closed models are accessed by API, where the provider handles infrastructure and updates. 'Open weight' isn't always 'open source,' since the training data and code may not be released.

**Customer line.** "Open-weight gives you maximum control but you run everything. Closed models are managed for you and are often the most capable. It's a classic control vs convenience tradeoff."

**Follow-up:** _Isn't self-hosting an open model more secure?_ It can keep data fully in-house, but security then depends on your own team. Many enterprises get similar assurance from a provider's data terms, regional hosting, and certifications.

**Myth:** Open-weight models are always free to run. **Fact:** The download may be free, but the servers, GPUs, and staff to run them are not.

Sources: [Microsoft Learn: Introduction to generative AI and agents](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/training/modules/fundamentals-generative-ai/)

### How models read and write

#### Next-token prediction

- [ ] Approved

**In a few words.** LLMs write by repeatedly predicting the most fitting next token

**Plain English.** Under the hood, a language model writes one small piece of text at a time, each time predicting what should come next given everything before it.

**Under the hood.** During pretraining the model learns to predict the next token in huge amounts of text. When generating, it repeats that step: predict a token, add it, predict again. Fine-tuning and RLHF shape which continuations it prefers, like following instructions.

**Customer line.** "At its core the model predicts the next word over and over. Training is what makes those predictions useful, accurate, and on-task."

**Follow-up:** _If it's just predicting words, how can it reason?_ Predicting well across billions of examples ends up requiring a lot of pattern-learning that looks like reasoning. It's impressive, but it's also why it can sound confident when it's wrong.

**Myth:** Because it only predicts the next word, an LLM can't do anything sophisticated. **Fact:** Next-token prediction at huge scale produces strong reasoning, writing, and coding abilities, even though errors still happen.

Sources: [Anthropic glossary: Pretraining](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/glossary)

#### Max output tokens _(also: max_tokens)_

- [ ] Approved

**In a few words.** The cap on how long a model's answer can be

**Plain English.** A limit on how much the model can write in one response. If an answer hits the limit, it gets cut off mid-thought.

**Under the hood.** Developers set a maximum number of output tokens per request, up to the model's own ceiling. Output tokens cost more than input tokens, so the cap is also a cost control. Thinking tokens on reasoning models count toward it too.

**Customer line.** "There's a cap on answer length per request, which keeps costs predictable. Long reports are usually built in sections."

**Follow-up:** _Why did an answer stop halfway?_ It probably hit the output cap. The product can raise the cap or continue the answer in a second request.

**Myth:** Context window and max output are the same limit. **Fact:** The context window covers everything in and out; max output caps only the response.

Sources: [Anthropic: Context windows](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/context-windows)

#### Streaming

- [ ] Approved

**In a few words.** Showing the answer as it's written instead of all at once

**Plain English.** Displaying the AI's answer word by word as it's generated, like watching someone type, instead of waiting for the whole thing.

**Under the hood.** With streaming, the API sends tokens as they're produced. It doesn't make the total answer faster, but it cuts the wait before something appears (time to first token), which makes chat interfaces feel much more responsive.

**Customer line.** "Streaming is why the answer starts appearing right away. The total time is similar, but it feels much faster."

**Follow-up:** _Does streaming change the answer?_ No. It's the same answer, just delivered as it's written.

**Myth:** Streaming makes the model think faster. **Fact:** It only shows the output sooner; generation speed is the same.

Sources: [Anthropic: Streaming messages](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/streaming)

#### Structured output _(also: JSON mode)_

- [ ] Approved

**In a few words.** Making the AI answer in a fixed, machine-readable format

**Plain English.** Having the AI respond in an exact format, like a form with specific fields, so other software can use the answer directly instead of parsing free text.

**Under the hood.** Developers provide a schema (for example, JSON with named fields) and the API constrains the model's response to match it. This makes AI reliable inside workflows: extracted fields go straight into a CRM, scores into a dashboard.

**Customer line.** "Structured output means the AI fills in a defined form, so its answers drop straight into your systems."

**Follow-up:** _Does structured output mean the content is correct?_ No. It guarantees the format, not the accuracy of what's in each field.

**Myth:** If the format is right, the data is right. **Fact:** Structured output fixes the shape of the answer; the values still need checking.

Sources: [Anthropic: Structured outputs](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/structured-outputs)

#### Token

- [ ] Approved

**In a few words.** The word-chunks models read, write, and bill by

**Plain English.** The chunks models read and write in: a whole word, part of a word, or punctuation. Models count, limit, and charge by tokens, not words.

**Under the hood.** Text is split into tokens before the model processes it. The ratio of characters to tokens varies by model and language. API pricing is usually per million input tokens and per million output tokens.

**Customer line.** "Tokens are how AI usage gets metered, like minutes on a phone plan. Longer documents and longer answers use more tokens."

**Follow-up:** _How many tokens is a typical document?_ It varies by model and language, so the safe answer is to measure it. Most providers offer a token-counting tool, and a quick test on real documents gives you a real number for budgeting.

**Myth:** One token equals one word. **Fact:** Tokens are often pieces of words, and the ratio differs by model and language. Never quote a fixed number without checking.

Sources: [Anthropic glossary: Tokens](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/glossary)

#### Context window

- [ ] Approved

**In a few words.** How much text the model can see at once

**Plain English.** How much text the model can 'see' at once: your question, the conversation so far, and any documents you give it. Think of it as the model's working memory, not its long-term knowledge.

**Under the hood.** The context window is measured in tokens and includes both input and output. Anything outside it is invisible to the model on that request. It's different from the training data, which is baked into the model's parameters.

**Customer line.** "The context window is how much the AI can read in one sitting. A bigger window means it can work across longer contracts or more call notes at once."

**Follow-up:** _So with a huge context window, will it remember our past conversations?_ Not automatically. Each request only sees what's sent in that request. 'Memory' across sessions is a product feature built on top, not part of the context window.

**Myth:** Bigger context windows mean the model remembers past conversations. **Fact:** The window only covers what's sent in the current request. Remembering across sessions requires a separate memory feature.

Sources: [Anthropic glossary: Context window](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/glossary)

### Risks and quality

#### Benchmark

- [ ] Approved

**In a few words.** A standard public test used to compare AI models

**Plain English.** A public set of test questions that labs use to compare models, like a standardized exam. Model launches usually come with benchmark scores.

**Under the hood.** Benchmarks cover areas like knowledge, math, coding, and reasoning. They're useful for rough comparisons but can be gamed or leak into training data, and they rarely match a specific company's tasks, which is why custom evals matter.

**Customer line.** "Benchmarks are a useful headline, but the test that matters is how the model does on your own examples."

**Follow-up:** _Model A beats model B on benchmarks. Should we pick A?_ Use it as a starting point, then test both on your tasks. Small benchmark gaps often don't show up in real work.

**Myth:** The model with the highest benchmark score is best for every job. **Fact:** Benchmarks measure general skills; your tasks may favor a different model.

Sources: [Anthropic: Define success criteria and build evaluations](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/test-and-evaluate/develop-tests)

#### Jailbreak

- [ ] Approved

**In a few words.** Tricking an AI into ignoring its safety rules

**Plain English.** When a user deliberately crafts a prompt to get an AI to break its rules, for example by role-playing a scenario where the rules supposedly don't apply.

**Under the hood.** Anthropic groups jailbreaks with direct prompt injection: the user is the adversary. Mitigations include screening inputs with a lightweight model, hardened system prompts, monitoring, and handling repeat offenders. Indirect prompt injection is the related risk where the attack hides in content the AI reads.

**Customer line.** "Jailbreaks are attempts to talk the AI out of its rules. Good products layer defenses so one clever prompt can't do real damage."

**Follow-up:** _Can jailbreaks be fully prevented?_ Not completely, so the strongest protection limits what the AI can access and do even if a jailbreak works.

**Myth:** Jailbreaking only matters for consumer chatbots. **Fact:** Any AI with access to data or actions needs defenses, since a jailbroken business tool can do more harm.

Sources: [Anthropic: Mitigate jailbreaks and prompt injections](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/test-and-evaluate/strengthen-guardrails/mitigate-jailbreaks)

#### Bias (in AI) _(also: algorithmic bias)_

- [ ] Approved

**In a few words.** AI treating groups unfairly because of patterns in its data

**Plain English.** When an AI system produces unfair or skewed results for certain groups, usually because the data it learned from reflected existing imbalances.

**Under the hood.** Bias can enter through training data, labels, design choices, or how a system is used. It matters most in high-stakes decisions like hiring or lending. Testing outputs across groups, documenting limits, and human review are standard mitigations.

**Customer line.** "Every model can carry bias from its data. What matters is whether the vendor tests for it and keeps people in the loop on high-stakes decisions."

**Follow-up:** _Is your product biased?_ Give the honest answer: describe what testing exists. No model is bias-free, and claiming otherwise backfires.

**Myth:** AI is objective because it's math. **Fact:** AI learns from human-generated data and can reproduce human biases.

Sources: [Microsoft Learn: Identify guiding principles for responsible AI](https://learn.microsoft.com/training/modules/embrace-responsible-ai-principles-practices/3-identify-guiding-principles-responsible-ai)

#### Red teaming

- [ ] Approved

**In a few words.** Deliberately attacking an AI to find its weaknesses

**Plain English.** Having people (or other AIs) try hard to make a system misbehave, leak data, or produce harmful output, so problems get found and fixed before launch.

**Under the hood.** Red teaming borrows from security testing. For AI it covers jailbreaks, prompt injection, data leakage, harmful content, and misuse. Anthropic recommends red-teaming your own agent with documents and tool outputs that deliberately contain injection attempts.

**Customer line.** "We attack our own system before anyone else can, so weaknesses are found in testing, not by customers."

**Follow-up:** _Can we see results of your red teaming?_ Ask what your company can share. Often a summary or a security review session is possible under NDA.

**Myth:** Red teaming is a one-time launch checklist. **Fact:** New attacks appear constantly, so red teaming works best as an ongoing practice.

Sources: [Anthropic: Mitigate jailbreaks and prompt injections](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/test-and-evaluate/strengthen-guardrails/mitigate-jailbreaks); [NIST AI Risk Management Framework](https://www.nist.gov/itl/ai-risk-management-framework)

#### LLM as a judge _(also: model-graded eval)_

- [ ] Approved

**In a few words.** Using one AI model to grade another's answers

**Plain English.** Using an AI model to score answers against a rubric, for things like tone or completeness that simple code can't check, so evaluations can scale beyond what people can grade by hand.

**Under the hood.** Anthropic's eval guidance recommends automating grading where possible, using code for exact checks and model-based grading for judgment calls, ideally with a different model or call than the one being graded. Grader prompts need clear rubrics, and their scores should be spot-checked by people.

**Customer line.** "We use AI to grade thousands of test answers quickly, and people spot-check the grader to keep it honest."

**Follow-up:** _Isn't it risky to have AI grade AI?_ It can be biased, which is why rubrics are explicit and humans regularly check a sample of the grades.

**Myth:** AI grading is as reliable as expert human review. **Fact:** It's fast and scalable but imperfect; people still need to check samples.

Sources: [Anthropic: Define success criteria and build evaluations](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/test-and-evaluate/develop-tests)

#### Model drift _(also: data drift)_

- [ ] Approved

> Needs a better source: the cited page is a general course, not a direct definition of this term.

**In a few words.** AI quality slipping as the world or the model changes

**Plain English.** When an AI system's results get worse over time, because the real world changed (new products, new customer behavior) or the underlying model was updated.

**Under the hood.** Predictive models drift when incoming data stops resembling training data. LLM products can also shift when a provider updates or retires a model. Ongoing monitoring and re-running evals after changes catch it.

**Customer line.** "AI quality isn't set-and-forget. We monitor results and re-test whenever the model or your data changes."

**Follow-up:** _How would we notice drift?_ Track a few quality metrics over time and re-run the same test set after any model update.

**Myth:** Once an AI system works, it keeps working the same way forever. **Fact:** Changes in data or the underlying model can quietly change results, so monitoring matters.

Sources: [NIST AI Risk Management Framework](https://www.nist.gov/itl/ai-risk-management-framework)

#### Explainability _(also: interpretability, XAI)_

- [ ] Approved

**In a few words.** Being able to say why an AI produced a result

**Plain English.** How well people can understand why an AI made a particular decision or gave a particular answer. It matters most when results affect people.

**Under the hood.** Some models are inherently easier to explain than others. For LLM products, practical explainability comes from citations, visible reasoning summaries, logs, and documentation. Interpretability research tries to understand what happens inside the model itself.

**Customer line.** "For anything that affects a person, you should be able to explain the result. Citations and audit logs are how we make that practical."

**Follow-up:** _Can you explain exactly why the model said that?_ Not at the level of every internal calculation, but we can show the sources, inputs, and steps it used.

**Myth:** Large language models can always explain their own reasoning accurately. **Fact:** A model's explanation of itself isn't guaranteed to reflect how it actually produced the answer.

Sources: [Microsoft Learn: Identify guiding principles for responsible AI](https://learn.microsoft.com/training/modules/embrace-responsible-ai-principles-practices/3-identify-guiding-principles-responsible-ai)

#### Deepfake _(also: synthetic media)_

- [ ] Approved

> Needs a better source: the cited page is a general course, not a direct definition of this term.

**In a few words.** Realistic fake audio, images, or video made with AI

**Plain English.** AI-generated media that convincingly imitates a real person, like a fake voice message from an executive asking for a wire transfer.

**Under the hood.** Generative models can now clone voices and faces from short samples. Defenses include verification procedures for sensitive requests, content provenance and watermarking standards, and disclosure rules for AI-generated content.

**Customer line.** "Deepfakes are why sensitive requests should always be verified through a second channel, no matter how real they sound."

**Follow-up:** _Can deepfakes be detected reliably?_ Not reliably by eye or ear. Process controls, like call-back verification, are more dependable than detection.

**Myth:** You can always spot a deepfake if you look closely. **Fact:** Good deepfakes are very hard to spot, so verification procedures matter more than detection.

Sources: [EUR-Lex: Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 (AI Act)](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj)

#### Data poisoning

- [ ] Approved

**In a few words.** Corrupting the data an AI learns from or reads

**Plain English.** An attack where someone slips bad or malicious content into data an AI learns from or searches, so it gives wrong or manipulated answers later.

**Under the hood.** Poisoning can target training or fine-tuning data, or the knowledge base a RAG system retrieves from. OWASP lists data and model poisoning among its top LLM application risks. Defenses include controlling who can add content, reviewing sources, and monitoring outputs.

**Customer line.** "If the AI answers from your knowledge base, then who can edit that knowledge base is a security question."

**Follow-up:** _How would poisoning happen to us?_ For example, an outsider editing a shared document the AI searches. Access controls on source content are the main defense.

**Myth:** Only the AI model itself can be attacked. **Fact:** The data it learns from or retrieves is often the easier target.

Sources: [OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications](https://owasp.org/www-project-top-10-for-large-language-model-applications/)

#### Hallucination

- [ ] Approved

**In a few words.** When AI states something false as if it were true

**Plain English.** When an AI states something false or made up as if it were true, like a fake statistic or a citation that doesn't exist.

**Under the hood.** Models generate likely-sounding text, not verified facts, so they can produce confident errors. You reduce it by letting the model say 'I don't know,' grounding answers in provided documents, requiring citations, and verifying claims. You can't eliminate it entirely.

**Customer line.** "No AI is hallucination-free, and anyone who says otherwise is overselling. What matters is how a product reduces it: grounding in your data, showing sources, and keeping a human on important calls."

**Follow-up:** _How do I know when it's making something up?_ Look for products that cite their sources so you can click through and check. If an answer can't point to where it came from, treat it as a draft.

**Myth:** Hallucinations only happen with cheap or old models. **Fact:** Even the best models still hallucinate sometimes. Techniques like grounding reduce it but don't eliminate it.

Sources: [Anthropic: Reduce hallucinations](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/test-and-evaluate/strengthen-guardrails/reduce-hallucinations)

#### Evals (evaluations)

- [ ] Approved

**In a few words.** Tests that measure AI quality on realistic examples

**Plain English.** Tests that measure how well an AI system performs on realistic examples, so you're judging it on evidence, not vibes.

**Under the hood.** An eval set mirrors real tasks, including edge cases, and grades the outputs by code (exact match, similarity), by a separate model acting as grader, or by people. Teams rerun evals whenever they change a prompt or model, to catch regressions.

**Customer line.** "Before we change anything in the AI, we run it against a test set of real examples, so improvements are measured, not guessed."

**Follow-up:** _Can we test it on our own data before buying?_ That's the best way to evaluate. Agree up front on a set of real examples and what 'good' looks like, then score the results.

**Myth:** Public benchmark scores tell you how a model will do on your tasks. **Fact:** Benchmarks are a rough guide. Testing on your own examples is far more predictive.

Sources: [Anthropic: Define success criteria and build evaluations](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/test-and-evaluate/develop-tests)

#### Prompt injection

- [ ] Approved

**In a few words.** Hidden instructions that trick the AI into breaking its rules

**Plain English.** An attack where someone hides instructions in text the AI reads, like an email or webpage, to trick it into ignoring its rules or leaking information.

**Under the hood.** OWASP ranks it as the top risk for LLM applications. Direct injection comes from the user's own message. Indirect injection hides in content the AI processes, like documents, websites, or tool results. It's especially risky for agents that can take actions. Defenses include limiting permissions, separating instructions from data, and requiring approval for sensitive actions.

**Customer line.** "Prompt injection is the AI version of phishing. The strongest defense isn't a clever prompt, it's limiting what the AI is allowed to access and do."

**Follow-up:** _Can it be fully prevented?_ Not completely today. That's why good products limit the damage: least-privilege access, human approval for risky actions, and monitoring.

**Myth:** Prompt injection only happens when a user types something malicious. **Fact:** The indirect kind hides in documents, emails, or web pages the AI reads, with no bad user involved.

Sources: [OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications](https://owasp.org/www-project-top-10-for-large-language-model-applications/)

### Kinds of AI

#### Supervised learning

- [ ] Approved

**In a few words.** Learning from examples that come with the right answers

**Plain English.** Training a model on examples that already have the right answer attached, like emails labeled 'spam' or 'not spam', so it learns to predict that answer for new examples.

**Under the hood.** Each training example pairs an input with a label. The model adjusts its parameters to reduce its errors on those labels. It powers most classic business ML: fraud detection, churn prediction, lead scoring, and document classification.

**Customer line.** "Supervised learning learns from labeled history, like past tickets tagged by category, then applies that to new ones."

**Follow-up:** _Do we need to label our own data?_ For custom predictions, often yes. Generative AI can sometimes skip that step because it already learned general skills.

**Myth:** Supervised means a person supervises every prediction. **Fact:** It means the training data came with correct answers, not that someone watches the model live.

Sources: [Google Machine Learning Glossary](https://developers.google.com/machine-learning/glossary)

#### Unsupervised learning

- [ ] Approved

**In a few words.** Finding patterns in data that has no labels

**Plain English.** Letting a model find structure in data on its own, without being told the right answers. For example, grouping customers into segments nobody defined in advance.

**Under the hood.** Common uses include clustering (grouping similar items), anomaly detection (spotting unusual activity), and learning compact representations like embeddings. Pretraining an LLM on raw text is closely related: the 'label' is just the next word in the text itself.

**Customer line.** "Unsupervised learning finds patterns you didn't know to look for, like natural customer segments or unusual activity."

**Follow-up:** _How do we know the groups it finds are meaningful?_ A person still has to interpret them. The model finds patterns; deciding if they matter is a business judgment.

**Myth:** Unsupervised means the AI runs with no human oversight. **Fact:** It refers to unlabeled training data, not to how the system is governed.

Sources: [Google Machine Learning Glossary](https://developers.google.com/machine-learning/glossary)

#### Reinforcement learning _(also: RL)_

- [ ] Approved

**In a few words.** Learning by trial and error from rewards

**Plain English.** A way of training where the model tries things and gets rewarded for good outcomes, gradually learning what works, like learning a game by playing it many times.

**Under the hood.** An agent takes actions, receives a reward signal, and updates its behavior to get more reward over time. It's used in game-playing AI and robotics, and in language models through RLHF and similar methods that reward better answers.

**Customer line.** "Reinforcement learning is learning from feedback on results. It's part of how chat models were trained to give helpful answers."

**Follow-up:** _Does the product learn from our feedback that way?_ Usually not live. Most products collect feedback, and the vendor may use it to improve future versions under their data terms.

**Myth:** Reinforcement learning means the AI improves every time you click thumbs up. **Fact:** Training happens offline, and whether your feedback is used at all depends on the vendor's data terms.

Sources: [Google Machine Learning Glossary](https://developers.google.com/machine-learning/glossary)

#### Predictive AI _(also: traditional AI, classification)_

- [ ] Approved

> Needs a better source: the cited page is a general course, not a direct definition of this term.

**In a few words.** AI that forecasts or classifies instead of generating content

**Plain English.** AI that answers 'what's likely?' or 'which bucket?': churn risk, fraud scores, lead scores, demand forecasts. It outputs a prediction, not new text.

**Under the hood.** Predictive models are usually trained with supervised learning on historical data and output a label, score, or number. They're often cheaper, faster, and easier to measure than generative models, and many products combine both.

**Customer line.** "Predictive AI scores and sorts; generative AI drafts and explains. Many good products use both together."

**Follow-up:** _Which one do we need?_ Depends on the job. If you need a risk score, predictive. If you need a summary or a draft, generative.

**Myth:** Generative AI has replaced predictive AI. **Fact:** Predictive models are still the right tool for scoring, forecasting, and classification at scale.

Sources: [Microsoft Learn: Introduction to generative AI and agents](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/training/modules/fundamentals-generative-ai/)

#### Natural language processing _(also: NLP)_

- [ ] Approved

**In a few words.** AI that works with human language, written or spoken

**Plain English.** The field of AI focused on human language: understanding it, translating it, summarizing it, and generating it.

**Under the hood.** Classic NLP tasks include sentiment analysis, entity extraction, translation, and classification. LLMs now handle most of these with one general model instead of a separate model per task.

**Customer line.** "NLP is AI for language. LLMs are the latest, most capable form of it."

**Follow-up:** _Is NLP different from what LLMs do?_ LLMs are part of NLP. Older NLP tools did one narrow task each; LLMs do many.

**Myth:** NLP is an old technology that LLMs made irrelevant. **Fact:** LLMs are the newest generation of NLP, not a replacement for the field.

Sources: [Microsoft Learn: AI fluency, Explore generative AI](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/training/modules/explore-generative-ai/)

#### Computer vision

- [ ] Approved

**In a few words.** AI that understands images and video

**Plain English.** AI that interprets images and video: reading text from a photo, spotting objects, or describing a chart.

**Under the hood.** Tasks include image classification, object detection, and optical character recognition (OCR). Multimodal LLMs can now read screenshots, documents, and charts directly, though accuracy varies with image quality.

**Customer line.** "Computer vision lets AI read what's in an image, like pulling numbers off a scanned invoice."

**Follow-up:** _How accurate is it on messy scans?_ It varies. Test on a sample of your real documents before relying on it.

**Myth:** If AI can read clean images, it'll read any image just as well. **Fact:** Blurry, handwritten, or cluttered images still trip up vision models.

Sources: [Anthropic: Vision](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/vision)

#### Speech recognition _(also: speech-to-text, transcription)_

- [ ] Approved

> Needs a better source: the cited page is a general course, not a direct definition of this term.

**In a few words.** Turning spoken audio into text

**Plain English.** AI that converts speech into written text, like call transcripts or voice dictation on your phone.

**Under the hood.** Speech-to-text models turn audio into text, which an LLM can then summarize or analyze. Accuracy depends on audio quality, accents, crosstalk, and specialized vocabulary. Many call-recording tools combine both steps.

**Customer line.** "Speech recognition turns your calls into text, and the language model turns that text into summaries and next steps."

**Follow-up:** _Will it get our product names right?_ Often not at first. Many tools let you add custom vocabulary to fix that.

**Myth:** A transcript is a perfect record of the call. **Fact:** Transcripts contain errors, especially with names, jargon, and people talking over each other.

Sources: [Microsoft Learn: AI fluency, Explore generative AI](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/training/modules/explore-generative-ai/)

#### Text-to-speech _(also: TTS, voice synthesis)_

- [ ] Approved

> Needs a better source: the cited page is a general course, not a direct definition of this term.

**In a few words.** Turning written text into natural-sounding speech

**Plain English.** AI that reads text out loud in a natural-sounding voice. It powers voice assistants and AI phone agents.

**Under the hood.** Modern TTS produces expressive, human-like voices and can sometimes clone a specific voice from a sample. Combined with speech recognition and an LLM, it enables voice agents that hold spoken conversations.

**Customer line.** "Text-to-speech gives the AI a voice, which is what makes AI phone agents possible."

**Follow-up:** _Should customers be told they're talking to AI?_ Yes. Many regulations, including the EU AI Act, expect people to be told when they're interacting with AI.

**Myth:** AI voices are easy to tell apart from real people. **Fact:** Good synthetic voices can be hard to distinguish, which is why disclosure matters.

### How models are made

#### Training data

- [ ] Approved

**In a few words.** The examples a model learns from

**Plain English.** Everything a model learned from: for an LLM, a huge collection of text and code, and for a custom model, a company's historical examples.

**Under the hood.** Training data shapes what a model knows, how it writes, and its blind spots and biases. Its quality, coverage, and licensing all matter. It's separate from the data you send while using the product, which may or may not be used for future training depending on the terms.

**Customer line.** "Training data is what the model learned from in the past. Your data, when you use the product, is a separate thing with its own rules."

**Follow-up:** _What was the model trained on?_ Providers usually describe their data broadly (public web data, licensed data, and so on). For specifics, point to the provider's model documentation.

**Myth:** Your company's data is automatically part of the model's training data. **Fact:** Not unless the terms allow it. Business agreements commonly exclude customer data from training.

Sources: [Anthropic glossary: Pretraining](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/glossary)

#### Data labeling _(also: annotation)_

- [ ] Approved

**In a few words.** Tagging examples with correct answers for training

**Plain English.** Having people (or AI) tag examples with the right answer, like marking tickets by category or rating which of two AI answers is better, so a model can learn from them.

**Under the hood.** Labeled data drives supervised learning, RLHF preference ratings, and evaluation sets. Label quality directly limits model quality, so labeling guidelines and consistency matter. LLMs increasingly help with labeling, with people checking samples.

**Customer line.** "Good labels make good models. A small, carefully labeled set of your examples is also the best way to test any AI vendor."

**Follow-up:** _How many labeled examples do we need?_ For testing a vendor, often 50 to a few hundred real examples is enough to see a clear picture.

**Myth:** More data always beats better data. **Fact:** A smaller set of clean, consistent labels often beats a larger messy one.

Sources: [Google Machine Learning Glossary](https://developers.google.com/machine-learning/glossary)

#### Synthetic data

- [ ] Approved

> Needs a better source: the cited page is a general course, not a direct definition of this term.

**In a few words.** Artificially generated data used for training or testing

**Plain English.** Data created by a program or another AI instead of collected from the real world, used to train or test models when real data is scarce or sensitive.

**Under the hood.** Synthetic data can fill gaps, create edge cases, or stand in for private data in testing. Its risk is that it can miss real-world messiness or copy the generating model's mistakes, so it's usually mixed with real data and checked.

**Customer line.** "Synthetic data lets you test AI safely without exposing real customer records, though final checks should still use real-world examples."

**Follow-up:** _Is synthetic data a privacy fix?_ It can reduce exposure, but poorly made synthetic data can still leak details from the real data it was based on.

**Myth:** Synthetic data is just as good as real data. **Fact:** It's useful, but it can miss the quirks of real data, so results should be confirmed on real examples.

Sources: [Google Machine Learning Glossary](https://developers.google.com/machine-learning/glossary)

#### Overfitting

- [ ] Approved

**In a few words.** Memorizing training examples instead of learning general patterns

**Plain English.** When a model learns its training examples too literally, like a student memorizing the practice test, and then does poorly on new examples.

**Under the hood.** An overfit model scores well on training data but poorly on data it hasn't seen. Teams guard against it by holding back test data, using more varied training data, and stopping training at the right time.

**Customer line.** "Overfitting is why we test on examples the model never saw. Great results on training data alone don't prove much."

**Follow-up:** _How would we know if a vendor's model is overfit?_ Test it on your own fresh examples. If results drop sharply compared with their demo, that's a warning sign.

**Myth:** A model that scores perfectly in training is ready for production. **Fact:** Perfect training scores can signal memorization. Performance on new data is what counts.

Sources: [Google Machine Learning Glossary](https://developers.google.com/machine-learning/glossary)

#### Alignment

- [ ] Approved

**In a few words.** Making AI behave in line with human intentions and values

**Plain English.** The work of making AI systems do what people actually intend, helpfully and safely, rather than something technically correct but harmful or unwanted.

**Under the hood.** Alignment techniques include RLHF, Constitutional AI, careful fine-tuning, and testing for harmful behavior. It's both a research field and a practical concern: an aligned assistant follows instructions, admits uncertainty, and refuses dangerous requests.

**Customer line.** "Alignment is the work that makes a model helpful, honest, and safe, not just capable."

**Follow-up:** _Is an aligned model guaranteed to be safe?_ No. Alignment greatly reduces risk, but products still need guardrails, monitoring, and human review for high-stakes uses.

**Myth:** Alignment is only about science-fiction risks. **Fact:** It also covers everyday behavior, like following instructions and not making things up.

Sources: [Anthropic glossary: HHH](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/glossary)

#### Constitutional AI

- [ ] Approved

**In a few words.** Training AI to follow written principles using AI feedback

**Plain English.** Anthropic's method of training a model to be helpful and harmless using a written set of principles (a 'constitution'), with the model critiquing and improving its own answers.

**Under the hood.** In a supervised stage the model critiques and revises its own outputs against the principles, then is fine-tuned on the revisions. In a reinforcement stage, an AI model (rather than human raters) judges which of two responses better follows the principles, an approach called RLAIF. It needs far fewer human labels than RLHF.

**Customer line.** "Constitutional AI trains the model against a written set of principles, which makes its intended behavior more transparent."

**Follow-up:** _Does that replace human oversight?_ No. It reduces how many human labels training needs; people still write the principles and test the results.

**Myth:** Constitutional AI means the model follows a legal constitution. **Fact:** The 'constitution' is a set of written principles used during training.

Sources: [Anthropic: Constitutional AI, Harmlessness from AI Feedback](https://www.anthropic.com/research/constitutional-ai-harmlessness-from-ai-feedback)

#### Distillation _(also: knowledge distillation)_

- [ ] Approved

> Needs a better source: the cited page is a general course, not a direct definition of this term.

**In a few words.** Training a small model to imitate a bigger one

**Plain English.** Teaching a smaller model to copy a larger model's answers, so you get much of the big model's quality at a fraction of the cost and speed.

**Under the hood.** The large 'teacher' model generates outputs, and the small 'student' model is trained to reproduce them. It's a common way to build fast, cheap models for specific tasks. Provider terms may restrict using their outputs to train competing models.

**Customer line.** "Distillation is how teams get a fast, cheap model that's nearly as good as a big one on a specific job."

**Follow-up:** _Can we distill the vendor's model ourselves?_ Check the terms. Many providers restrict using their outputs to train other models.

**Myth:** A distilled model is as capable as its teacher everywhere. **Fact:** It's usually close on the tasks it was trained for and weaker elsewhere.

Sources: [Google Machine Learning Glossary](https://developers.google.com/machine-learning/glossary)

#### Quantization

- [ ] Approved

**In a few words.** Shrinking a model by storing its numbers with less precision

**Plain English.** Making a model smaller and faster by storing its internal numbers more roughly, a bit like saving a photo at lower resolution. It usually costs a little quality.

**Under the hood.** Parameters stored as 16- or 32-bit numbers are converted to 8-bit or 4-bit, cutting memory and speeding up inference. It's common for running open-weight models on smaller hardware. The same idea applies to embeddings, where it shrinks storage for search indexes.

**Customer line.** "Quantization trades a little accuracy for a lot of speed and lower hardware cost, which matters if you run models yourself."

**Follow-up:** _Will we notice the quality drop?_ Often not at moderate settings, but test on your tasks; aggressive quantization can hurt.

**Myth:** Quantization makes a model smarter because it's faster. **Fact:** It makes it smaller and faster, usually with a small quality cost.

Sources: [Anthropic: Embeddings (quantization options)](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/embeddings)

#### Pretraining

- [ ] Approved

**In a few words.** The first huge phase where a model reads lots of text

**Plain English.** The first, huge phase of building an LLM, where it reads an enormous amount of text and learns to predict the next word. That's where it picks up language, facts, and reasoning patterns.

**Under the hood.** Pretraining uses a large, mostly unlabeled body of text. The result is good at continuing text but not at following instructions, which is why fine-tuning and RLHF come after it.

**Customer line.** "Pretraining is where the model learns general knowledge. Everything after that is about making it helpful, safe, and good at specific jobs."

**Follow-up:** _Does that mean the model knows about events from last week?_ Not on its own. Its knowledge stops at a training cutoff date. For current information it needs a search tool or access to live data.

**Myth:** A pretrained model is ready to be a helpful chatbot. **Fact:** A raw pretrained model just continues text. It takes fine-tuning and RLHF to make it follow instructions.

Sources: [Anthropic glossary: Pretraining](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/glossary)

#### Fine-tuning

- [ ] Approved

**In a few words.** Extra training on specific examples to specialize a model

**Plain English.** Taking a model that's already trained and training it a bit more on a specific set of examples, so it gets better at one domain, task, or style.

**Under the hood.** Fine-tuning further trains a pretrained model on additional data, shifting its behavior toward the patterns in that data. It's slower and pricier to change than prompting or RAG, and the quality of the fine-tuning data matters a lot.

**Customer line.** "Fine-tuning changes the model itself. For most business use cases, connecting the model to your data (RAG) gets you there faster and is easier to keep up to date."

**Follow-up:** _Will you fine-tune a model on our data?_ Ask what problem fine-tuning would solve. If it's 'the model needs to know our documents,' retrieval is usually better. Fine-tuning fits better for a specific format, tone, or narrow task.

**Myth:** Fine-tuning is the best way to teach a model your company's facts. **Fact:** Facts change, and a fine-tuned model goes stale. RAG pulls in your current documents at question time instead.

Sources: [Anthropic glossary: Fine-tuning](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/glossary)

#### RLHF (Reinforcement learning from human feedback) _(also: RLHF)_

- [ ] Approved

**In a few words.** People rank answers so the model learns what good looks like

**Plain English.** A training step where people rank the model's answers from better to worse, and the model learns to give more answers like the good ones.

**Under the hood.** Human raters compare two or more model outputs. Reinforcement learning then pushes the model toward outputs like the higher-ranked ones. It's a big part of what turns a raw pretrained model into a helpful assistant.

**Customer line.** "RLHF is how models learn manners and judgment: humans grade the answers and the model learns what 'good' looks like."

**Follow-up:** _Does that make the model unbiased?_ No. It reflects the preferences of the people and guidelines that shaped it. It helps a lot, but it isn't a guarantee.

**Myth:** RLHF means humans review every answer the AI gives you. **Fact:** The human feedback happens during training. Your live answers aren't reviewed by a person unless the product adds a human-in-the-loop step.

Sources: [Anthropic glossary: RLHF](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/glossary)

### Working with models

#### Zero-shot prompting

- [ ] Approved

**In a few words.** Asking the model to do a task with no examples

**Plain English.** Giving the model a task with just instructions and no examples. Modern models handle many tasks well this way.

**Under the hood.** Zero-shot relies on the model's general training. When results are inconsistent or the format matters, adding a few examples (few-shot) usually helps. Clear instructions and context do most of the work either way.

**Customer line.** "Most tasks work with plain instructions. Where consistency matters, we add a few examples."

**Follow-up:** _When would you add examples?_ When the output needs a specific style or format, or when zero-shot results vary too much.

**Myth:** Zero-shot means the model has never seen anything like the task. **Fact:** It means no examples in the prompt; the model still relies on everything it learned in training.

Sources: [Anthropic: Prompting best practices](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/claude-prompting-best-practices)

#### Chain of thought _(also: CoT, step-by-step reasoning)_

- [ ] Approved

**In a few words.** Having the model work through steps before answering

**Plain English.** Getting the model to reason step by step before giving its final answer, which tends to improve accuracy on multi-step problems.

**Under the hood.** It started as a prompting trick ('think step by step') and is now built into reasoning models through thinking features. It helps most with math, analysis, and multi-step logic, at the cost of more tokens and time.

**Customer line.** "Chain of thought means the AI works through the problem before answering, which helps on anything with multiple steps."

**Follow-up:** _Can we see the reasoning?_ Some products show a summary of it. It's useful for spotting where an answer went wrong.

**Myth:** Step-by-step reasoning guarantees a correct answer. **Fact:** It improves accuracy on hard tasks, but the steps themselves can still be wrong.

Sources: [Anthropic: Thinking](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/thinking)

#### Prompt chaining

- [ ] Approved

**In a few words.** Breaking a task into steps, each with its own prompt

**Plain English.** Splitting a big task into smaller steps and running them one after another, with each step's output feeding the next, like 'summarize the call, then draft the follow-up email from the summary.'

**Under the hood.** Each link in the chain is a focused prompt, which makes results more reliable and easier to debug than one giant prompt. It's the simplest kind of AI workflow, before reaching for agents.

**Customer line.** "We break complex jobs into small, checkable steps. It's more reliable than asking for everything at once."

**Follow-up:** _Is that slower?_ Slightly, since there are several calls, but each step is easier to get right and to test.

**Myth:** One big detailed prompt always beats several smaller ones. **Fact:** Splitting complex tasks into steps is often more reliable and easier to fix.

Sources: [Anthropic: Building effective agents](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/building-effective-agents)

#### Role prompting

- [ ] Approved

**In a few words.** Telling the model what role or expertise to take on

**Plain English.** Telling the AI who to act as, like 'You are an experienced customer success manager', to shape its tone, focus, and level of detail.

**Under the hood.** A role in the system prompt steers vocabulary, priorities, and style. It works best combined with clear instructions and context about the audience and goal. It doesn't add knowledge the model doesn't have.

**Customer line.** "Giving the AI a role is like briefing a contractor on their job title. It shapes the tone and focus of the work."

**Follow-up:** _If we tell it to act as a lawyer, can we trust its legal advice?_ No. A role changes style and focus, not qualifications. Expert review is still needed.

**Myth:** Telling the AI it's an expert makes it an expert. **Fact:** It changes how it responds, not what it actually knows.

Sources: [Anthropic: Prompting best practices](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/claude-prompting-best-practices)

#### Context engineering _(also: context rot)_

- [ ] Approved

**In a few words.** Choosing exactly what information the model sees

**Plain English.** The work of deciding what goes into the model's context window, including instructions, documents, tool results, and history, so it has what it needs and nothing that distracts it.

**Under the hood.** Anthropic describes it as curating the optimal set of tokens the model sees at each step, a broader job than writing the prompt. It matters because models recall less reliably as context fills up ('context rot'), so more context isn't automatically better.

**Customer line.** "Giving the AI the right information matters more than giving it all the information. We curate what it sees."

**Follow-up:** _Why not just give it everything, since the window is huge?_ Too much irrelevant material makes recall less reliable and costs more. Focused context gets better answers.

**Myth:** Filling the context window with every document always helps. **Fact:** Models get less reliable as context grows with irrelevant material.

Sources: [Anthropic: Effective context engineering for AI agents](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/effective-context-engineering-for-ai-agents)

#### Prompt

- [ ] Approved

**In a few words.** The instructions and content you give the model

**Plain English.** Whatever you give the model to respond to: your question, your instructions, examples, or pasted documents.

**Under the hood.** A prompt is the full input sent to the model on a request, often split into a system prompt (standing instructions) and user messages. The model's output depends heavily on how clear and specific the prompt is.

**Customer line.** "The prompt is the brief you hand the AI. A clear brief gets a better result, just like with a new hire."

**Follow-up:** _Do my users need to learn to write good prompts?_ Good products do most of the prompting behind the scenes. Users still get better results when they're specific about what they want and give context.

**Myth:** There's a secret magic phrase that unlocks better AI answers. **Fact:** What works is clarity: say what you want, give context and examples, and describe the output format.

Sources: [Anthropic: Prompt engineering overview](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/overview)

#### System prompt

- [ ] Approved

**In a few words.** Standing instructions the product gives the AI behind the scenes

**Plain English.** Background instructions that the product gives the AI before you type anything, like a job description: who it is, what it should and shouldn't do, and how to respond.

**Under the hood.** The system prompt sits ahead of the conversation and shapes every response: role, tone, rules, available tools, and formatting. AI products put much of their behavior and guardrails here.

**Customer line.** "The system prompt is the AI's standing orders. It's one of the ways we keep it on-task and on-brand for your team."

**Follow-up:** _Can a user trick the AI into ignoring those instructions?_ It's a real risk called prompt injection. Good products don't rely on the system prompt alone. They also limit what the AI is allowed to access and do.

**Myth:** A system prompt is a security control that users can't get around. **Fact:** System prompts guide behavior but can be manipulated. Real security comes from permissions and limits outside the model.

Sources: [Anthropic: Prompt engineering overview](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/overview); [OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications](https://owasp.org/www-project-top-10-for-large-language-model-applications/)

#### Prompt engineering

- [ ] Approved

**In a few words.** Writing and testing prompts to get reliable output

**Plain English.** The skill of writing and testing prompts to get reliable, high-quality output from a model.

**Under the hood.** It covers techniques like clear instructions, examples (few-shot), structured formatting, assigning a role, letting the model think step by step, and chaining prompts together. It works best when you have success criteria and tests to measure against.

**Customer line.** "Prompt engineering is how we tune the AI's behavior without retraining it. It's fast to change and easy to test."

**Follow-up:** _Isn't prompt engineering just a fad?_ The 'magic words' side is fading as models improve. Writing clear instructions and testing them against real examples is lasting skill.

**Myth:** Prompt engineering can fix any AI problem. **Fact:** Some issues, like speed or cost, are better solved by choosing a different model, and some need better data or retrieval.

Sources: [Anthropic: Prompt engineering overview](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/overview)

#### Few-shot prompting _(also: multishot prompting, examples in the prompt)_

- [ ] Approved

**In a few words.** Showing the model a few examples before the task

**Plain English.** Showing the model a few examples of what you want before asking it to do the task. 'Zero-shot' means no examples.

**Under the hood.** Including a few input-and-output examples in the prompt steers the format, tone, and decisions of the response without any retraining. It's one of the most reliable prompting techniques.

**Customer line.** "Instead of retraining the AI, we just show it a few good examples, the way you'd train a new rep with sample emails."

**Follow-up:** _How many examples do you need?_ Often three to five varied examples are enough. The key is that they cover the different cases you care about.

**Myth:** To teach an AI a new format, you have to retrain it. **Fact:** A handful of examples in the prompt usually does the job.

Sources: [Anthropic: Prompt engineering overview](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/overview)

#### Temperature

- [ ] Approved

**In a few words.** A dial for how creative or predictable answers are

**Plain English.** A setting for how creative or predictable the model's answers are. Low temperature means safer and more consistent, and high means more varied and creative.

**Under the hood.** Temperature changes how the model picks among likely next tokens. Low values favor the most probable choice, and high values let less likely choices through. Even at zero, outputs aren't guaranteed to be identical every time.

**Customer line.** "For things like summaries and data extraction we keep the AI's 'creativity dial' low so answers are consistent."

**Follow-up:** _If you set it to zero, will I always get the exact same answer?_ Usually very similar, but not guaranteed identical. That's why important outputs should be checked, not assumed.

**Myth:** Temperature zero makes the model's answers fully deterministic and correct. **Fact:** It makes outputs more consistent, not more correct, and even then they can vary slightly.

Sources: [Anthropic glossary: Temperature](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/glossary)

#### Reasoning (thinking) models _(also: extended thinking, chain of thought)_

- [ ] Approved

**In a few words.** Models that think step by step before answering

**Plain English.** Models that work through a problem step by step before giving their final answer, which helps on harder tasks like math, analysis, and multi-step work.

**Under the hood.** With thinking turned on, the model writes out intermediate reasoning (trying approaches, checking work) before answering. That improves quality on complex tasks, but the thinking tokens are billed as output and add time.

**Customer line.** "For hard questions the AI can 'show its work' before answering. It's slower and costs a bit more, so we use it where accuracy matters most."

**Follow-up:** _Why not turn thinking on for everything?_ It adds cost and wait time. Simple tasks like a quick summary don't benefit much.

**Myth:** Reasoning models can't make mistakes because they check their work. **Fact:** They make fewer errors on complex tasks, but their reasoning can still be wrong.

Sources: [Anthropic: Thinking](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/thinking)

### Search and retrieval

#### Semantic search

- [ ] Approved

**In a few words.** Search that matches meaning, not just exact words

**Plain English.** Search that understands what you mean, so a question about 'customers leaving' also finds documents about 'churn' and 'cancellations'.

**Under the hood.** Documents and queries are turned into embeddings, and results are ranked by how close their meanings are. It's the retrieval step in most RAG systems, often combined with keyword search for exact terms like product codes.

**Customer line.** "Semantic search finds the right answer even when you don't use the exact words in the document."

**Follow-up:** _Does that replace keyword search?_ Usually they work together. Keywords are better for exact names and codes; meaning-based search is better for questions.

**Myth:** Semantic search always beats keyword search. **Fact:** For exact identifiers, like error codes or SKUs, keyword search often wins, which is why hybrid search exists.

Sources: [Anthropic: Embeddings](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/embeddings)

#### Chunking

- [ ] Approved

**In a few words.** Splitting documents into small pieces for search

**Plain English.** Cutting long documents into smaller passages before indexing them, so search can pull back just the relevant part instead of a whole manual.

**Under the hood.** Chunks are typically a few hundred tokens. Too small and they lose context; too large and they dilute relevance. Anthropic's 'contextual retrieval' adds a short, generated description of where each chunk fits in its document to improve search accuracy.

**Customer line.** "We split your documents into passages so the AI finds the exact paragraph that answers the question."

**Follow-up:** _Does how you split documents really matter?_ Yes. Chunking is one of the biggest levers on whether a RAG system finds the right answer.

**Myth:** RAG feeds the model your entire document library on every question. **Fact:** It retrieves a handful of relevant chunks, which is why chunking quality matters.

Sources: [Anthropic: Introducing Contextual Retrieval](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/contextual-retrieval)

#### Reranking _(also: reranker)_

- [ ] Approved

**In a few words.** A second pass that reorders search results by relevance

**Plain English.** After a fast first search pulls back many candidates, a second, more careful model reorders them so the most relevant ones go to the AI.

**Under the hood.** Rerankers score each candidate against the query more precisely than embeddings alone. In Anthropic's contextual retrieval experiments, adding reranking on top of contextual embeddings and BM25 cut retrieval failures by about 67% versus the baseline.

**Customer line.** "Reranking is a second check on search results, so the AI gets the best few passages instead of the first few."

**Follow-up:** _Does it slow things down?_ Slightly, but it usually improves answer quality and can lower cost by sending the model fewer, better passages.

**Myth:** The first search results are always the most relevant. **Fact:** Fast first-pass search is approximate; reranking often reorders it noticeably.

Sources: [Anthropic: Introducing Contextual Retrieval](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/contextual-retrieval)

#### Hybrid search _(also: BM25)_

- [ ] Approved

**In a few words.** Combining keyword search with meaning-based search

**Plain English.** Running keyword search and semantic search together and blending the results, so you catch both exact matches (like an error code) and related meanings.

**Under the hood.** Keyword ranking methods like BM25 excel at exact terms; embeddings excel at meaning. Hybrid search merges both result lists. Anthropic reported that combining contextual embeddings with contextual BM25 cut retrieval failures by 49%.

**Customer line.** "Hybrid search gets you the best of both: exact matches on names and codes, plus answers phrased differently than your question."

**Follow-up:** _Do we need to set that up?_ Usually the vendor handles it. It's a good question to ask about how their search works.

**Myth:** Keyword search is obsolete now that we have AI search. **Fact:** Keyword search is still essential for exact terms, which is why the best systems combine both.

Sources: [Anthropic: Introducing Contextual Retrieval](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/contextual-retrieval)

#### Citations

- [ ] Approved

**In a few words.** Showing exactly which source each part of an answer came from

**Plain English.** When the AI points to the specific document or passage behind each statement, so you can click through and check it.

**Under the hood.** Citation features link parts of a response to passages in the provided documents. They make answers auditable and are one of the most effective ways to catch hallucinations, because unsupported claims stand out.

**Customer line.** "Every answer shows its source, so your team can verify anything important in one click."

**Follow-up:** _Can a cited answer still be wrong?_ Yes, if the source is wrong or the summary misreads it. Citations make it easy to check, which is the point.

**Myth:** If an AI answer has a citation, it must be accurate. **Fact:** Citations make checking easy, but the claim still needs to match the source.

Sources: [Anthropic: Citations](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/citations)

#### Knowledge graph

- [ ] Approved

> Needs a better source: the cited page is a general course, not a direct definition of this term.

**In a few words.** A map of things and how they're connected

**Plain English.** A database that stores facts as connections, like 'Account A uses Product B' and 'Contact C works at Account A', so you can follow relationships.

**Under the hood.** Knowledge graphs represent entities and relationships explicitly. Some AI systems combine them with RAG ('GraphRAG') to answer questions that require connecting facts across many documents, which plain chunk search handles poorly.

**Customer line.** "A knowledge graph helps the AI connect the dots between people, accounts, and products instead of just finding matching text."

**Follow-up:** _Do we need a knowledge graph for AI search?_ Not usually to start. It helps most when questions depend on relationships across lots of records.

**Myth:** Every AI search system needs a knowledge graph. **Fact:** Most work fine with standard retrieval; graphs help with relationship-heavy questions.

Sources: [Google Machine Learning Glossary](https://developers.google.com/machine-learning/glossary)

#### Embeddings

- [ ] Approved

**In a few words.** Numbers that capture meaning so similar text can be found

**Plain English.** A way of turning text into a list of numbers that captures its meaning, so a computer can tell that 'car' and 'automobile' are close even though the words are different.

**Under the hood.** An embedding model converts text (or images) into a vector. Texts with similar meaning end up close together, so you can measure similarity with math. This powers semantic search, recommendations, and the retrieval step in RAG.

**Customer line.** "Embeddings let the system search by meaning, not just keywords, so asking about 'customers leaving' also finds notes that say 'churn.'"

**Follow-up:** _Is that the same as the chatbot model?_ No, it's usually a separate, smaller model built just for this. For example, Anthropic doesn't make one and points developers to providers like Voyage AI.

**Myth:** Embeddings are a compressed copy of your documents that can be read back. **Fact:** They're a numeric fingerprint of meaning, used for comparing and finding things, not for storing the full text.

Sources: [Anthropic: Embeddings](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/embeddings)

#### Vector database

- [ ] Approved

**In a few words.** A database that finds text closest in meaning

**Plain English.** A database built to store embeddings and quickly find the ones closest in meaning to a question.

**Under the hood.** Vector databases (or vector extensions like pgvector for Postgres) store vectors and run fast nearest-neighbor searches. In a RAG system, documents are embedded and stored, then the most relevant chunks are pulled back for each question.

**Customer line.** "The vector database is the library index that lets the AI find the right page of your documentation in a fraction of a second."

**Follow-up:** _Where is that database hosted, and who can access it?_ That's a fair data-security question. It contains a representation of your content, so it should get the same access controls and hosting terms as the original data.

**Myth:** You need a special standalone vector database product to do AI search. **Fact:** Many teams use a vector extension inside a database they already have, like Postgres with pgvector.

Sources: [Anthropic: Embeddings (RAG and vector search)](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/embeddings)

#### RAG (Retrieval-augmented generation) _(also: RAG, grounding)_

- [ ] Approved

**In a few words.** Looking up your documents first so answers are grounded in them

**Plain English.** Before answering, the system looks up relevant information from your documents and hands it to the AI, so the answer is based on your real content instead of just the model's memory.

**Under the hood.** When a question comes in, relevant passages are retrieved (often with embeddings and a vector database) and placed into the context window alongside the question. This grounds the answer in evidence, supports citations, and keeps answers current without retraining. Quality depends on what gets retrieved.

**Customer line.** "RAG means the AI answers from your knowledge base, not from the internet at large, and it can show you which document each answer came from."

**Follow-up:** _If our documents are wrong or outdated, what happens?_ The answers will be too. RAG is only as good as the content it retrieves, so it's worth cleaning up the knowledge base before launch.

**Myth:** RAG retrains the model on your documents. **Fact:** The model doesn't change. Relevant documents are fetched and shown to it at question time.

Sources: [Anthropic glossary: RAG](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/glossary); [Anthropic: Reduce hallucinations](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/test-and-evaluate/strengthen-guardrails/reduce-hallucinations)

### Agents

#### Agent loop _(also: agentic loop)_

- [ ] Approved

**In a few words.** Think, act with a tool, check the result, repeat

**Plain English.** The cycle an AI agent runs: decide what to do, use a tool, look at the result, and decide the next step, until the task is done.

**Under the hood.** Each turn, the model either calls a tool or finishes. The application runs the tool and returns the result, and the loop continues. Stopping rules, step limits, and approval steps keep loops from running away or taking risky actions.

**Customer line.** "An agent works in a loop, act, check, adjust, and we put limits and checkpoints on that loop."

**Follow-up:** _What stops an agent from looping forever?_ Step limits, budgets, and stopping rules set by the product, plus human approval for risky actions.

**Myth:** An agent plans everything up front and then executes. **Fact:** Agents typically adjust step by step based on what each tool call returns.

Sources: [Anthropic: How tool use works](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/agents-and-tools/tool-use/how-tool-use-works)

#### Multi-agent system _(also: orchestrator-worker)_

- [ ] Approved

**In a few words.** Several AI agents splitting up and coordinating a task

**Plain English.** Instead of one agent doing everything, a lead agent breaks the job into parts and hands them to helper agents that work in parallel, then combines their results.

**Under the hood.** Anthropic's research system uses an orchestrator-worker pattern and reported large gains on broad research tasks, but multi-agent systems used about 15 times the tokens of a normal chat. They suit high-value, parallelizable work, not tightly interdependent tasks.

**Customer line.** "Multi-agent setups are powerful for big research-style jobs, but they cost more, so we use them where the value justifies it."

**Follow-up:** _Why not use multiple agents for everything?_ They use far more tokens and add complexity. For most tasks, one agent or a simple workflow is better.

**Myth:** More agents always means better results. **Fact:** Multi-agent systems help on broad, parallel tasks but cost much more and can add coordination problems.

Sources: [Anthropic: How we built our multi-agent research system](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/multi-agent-research-system)

#### Agent memory _(also: memory)_

- [ ] Approved

**In a few words.** How an AI keeps information between conversations

**Plain English.** Ways for an AI to remember things beyond a single conversation, like your preferences or past decisions, since models don't remember on their own.

**Under the hood.** Models are stateless: each request only sees what's sent. Memory features store notes or summaries outside the model (in files or a database) and bring relevant pieces back into context later. What's stored, for how long, and who can see it are data-governance questions.

**Customer line.** "The model itself doesn't remember. Memory is a product feature that saves notes and brings them back, and you control what it keeps."

**Follow-up:** _Can we see and delete what it remembers?_ That's the right question to ask any vendor. Good memory features are visible and editable.

**Myth:** AI assistants naturally remember everything you've told them. **Fact:** Models are stateless; memory only exists if the product builds and stores it.

Sources: [Anthropic: Memory tool](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/agents-and-tools/tool-use/memory-tool)

#### Computer use _(also: browser use)_

- [ ] Approved

**In a few words.** AI operating a computer screen by clicking and typing

**Plain English.** Letting an AI control a computer like a person does: looking at screenshots, moving the mouse, clicking buttons, and typing, so it can use software that has no API.

**Under the hood.** The model receives screenshots and returns actions like clicks and keystrokes, which run in a controlled (ideally sandboxed) environment. It's slower and less reliable than direct integrations, and it raises prompt-injection risk from whatever appears on screen.

**Customer line.** "Computer use lets AI work in apps that have no integration, but direct integrations are faster and safer when they exist."

**Follow-up:** _Is it safe to let AI click around our systems?_ Only with tight limits: a sandboxed environment, restricted accounts, and approval for anything that changes data.

**Myth:** Computer use is the best way to connect AI to any app. **Fact:** It's a fallback for apps without APIs. Direct tool integrations are faster and more reliable.

Sources: [Anthropic: Computer use tool](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/agents-and-tools/tool-use/computer-use-tool)

#### Orchestration

- [ ] Approved

**In a few words.** Coordinating models, tools, and steps into one workflow

**Plain English.** The layer that coordinates everything in an AI system: which model handles which step, which tools get called, in what order, and what happens when something fails.

**Under the hood.** Orchestration can be fixed code (a workflow), model-directed (an agent), or a mix. It handles routing, retries, state, logging, and approval steps. Much of the real engineering in AI products lives here, not in the model.

**Customer line.** "The model is one piece. Orchestration is what turns it into a reliable workflow that fits your process."

**Follow-up:** _Is orchestration where vendors differ?_ Often, yes. Two products on the same model can behave very differently because of how they orchestrate it.

**Myth:** AI products are mostly just the model. **Fact:** Orchestration, data access, and controls around the model often matter as much as the model itself.

Sources: [Anthropic: Building effective agents](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/building-effective-agents)

#### Tool use _(also: function calling)_

- [ ] Approved

**In a few words.** Letting the AI call other software, like search or a CRM

**Plain English.** Letting the AI use other software, like searching the web, checking a calendar, or looking up a record in a CRM, instead of only writing text.

**Under the hood.** The developer describes available tools to the model. The model decides when a tool would help and returns a structured request (which tool, with what inputs). The application or the provider runs the tool and sends the result back, and the model uses it to answer.

**Customer line.** "Tool use is what lets the AI actually do things in your systems, not just talk about them, and you control which tools it's allowed to touch."

**Follow-up:** _Can it take actions without asking us first?_ Only if it's set up that way. Well-built products let you require approval for actions, especially anything that changes data or contacts customers.

**Myth:** When an AI uses a tool, the model itself is running the software. **Fact:** The model only asks for the tool call. Your application or the provider's servers actually run it.

Sources: [Anthropic: Tool use overview](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/agents-and-tools/tool-use/overview)

#### AI agent

- [ ] Approved

**In a few words.** AI that works toward a goal over multiple self-chosen steps

**Plain English.** An AI that works toward a goal across multiple steps, deciding for itself which tools to use and what to do next, instead of just answering one question.

**Under the hood.** Anthropic's definition: agents are systems where the model dynamically directs its own process and tool use. Agents trade higher cost and latency for flexibility, so they suit open-ended tasks. Well-defined tasks are often better served by a simpler fixed workflow.

**Customer line.** "A chatbot answers questions. An agent takes a goal, like 'prep me for my renewal call,' and works through the steps to get it done."

**Follow-up:** _Is every AI product with 'agent' in the name actually an agent?_ No. The term gets stretched. A useful test: does the AI choose its own next steps, or is it following a fixed script someone built?

**Myth:** An agent is always better than a simple AI workflow. **Fact:** Agents are slower and cost more. For predictable tasks, a fixed workflow is often more reliable.

Sources: [Anthropic: Building effective agents](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/building-effective-agents)

#### Workflow vs agent

- [ ] Approved

**In a few words.** Fixed steps set by a developer versus steps the AI picks

**Plain English.** In a workflow, a developer decides the steps in advance and the AI fills in each one. In an agent, the AI decides the steps itself.

**Under the hood.** Anthropic describes workflows as models and tools orchestrated through predefined code paths, and agents as systems where the model directs its own process. Their advice is to start with the simplest solution and add complexity only when it clearly helps.

**Customer line.** "For repeatable tasks, we use fixed workflows because they're predictable. We save agents for messier tasks where flexibility is worth it."

**Follow-up:** _Which one is more trustworthy?_ Workflows are easier to predict and audit. Agents are more flexible but need stronger guardrails and monitoring.

**Myth:** 'Agentic' just means the product uses AI. **Fact:** It specifically means the AI chooses its own steps and actions, which is a meaningful difference for risk and reliability.

Sources: [Anthropic: Building effective agents](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/building-effective-agents)

#### MCP (Model Context Protocol) _(also: MCP)_

- [ ] Approved

**In a few words.** An open standard for plugging AI into apps and data

**Plain English.** An open standard for plugging AI assistants into other apps and data, often described as 'USB-C for AI.' Build a connector once and any MCP-compatible AI can use it.

**Under the hood.** MCP standardizes how applications provide context and tools to models. An MCP server exposes data and actions (for example, a CRM or a file store), and MCP clients (AI apps) connect to it in a consistent way. Anthropic introduced it, and it's open for anyone to use.

**Customer line.** "MCP is a common plug format, so connecting AI to your systems doesn't need a custom integration for every tool."

**Follow-up:** _Does MCP make it secure by default?_ No. It standardizes the connection. You still need to control which servers you trust and what each one is allowed to access.

**Myth:** MCP only works with Claude. **Fact:** It's an open protocol meant to work across AI apps and models.

Sources: [Anthropic glossary: MCP](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/glossary); [Model Context Protocol docs](https://modelcontextprotocol.io/)

### Performance and cost

#### GPU (and AI compute) _(also: compute, accelerator)_

- [ ] Approved

> Needs a better source: the cited page is a general course, not a direct definition of this term.

**In a few words.** The chips that train and run AI models

**Plain English.** Graphics processing units: chips originally built for video games that turned out to be ideal for AI math. Training and running models takes huge numbers of them.

**Under the hood.** GPUs (and similar accelerators) do many calculations in parallel, which neural networks need. Compute supply and cost shape AI pricing, rate limits, and which models are practical to run. Self-hosting open-weight models means paying for this hardware yourself.

**Customer line.** "AI runs on expensive specialized chips. That's the real cost behind usage-based pricing."

**Follow-up:** _Why does AI pricing change so often?_ Hardware gets more efficient and models get optimized, so the cost to serve them keeps shifting.

**Myth:** Running AI is cheap once the model is trained. **Fact:** Every request uses real compute, which is why usage has a cost.

#### Rate limits

- [ ] Approved

**In a few words.** Caps on how much AI usage is allowed per minute

**Plain English.** Limits a provider puts on how many requests and tokens you can use per minute, to manage capacity and prevent abuse. Going over returns an error until capacity frees up.

**Under the hood.** Anthropic measures rate limits in requests per minute and input and output tokens per minute, by usage tier, and returns HTTP 429 when they're exceeded. Separate monthly spend limits cap total cost. Prompt caching effectively raises throughput, since cached input mostly doesn't count.

**Customer line.** "Rate limits are capacity caps per minute. For a big rollout, we plan capacity up front so your team never hits them."

**Follow-up:** _What happens if we hit a limit during a busy period?_ Requests get briefly rejected and retried. For large deployments, higher tiers or reserved capacity avoid that.

**Myth:** Rate limits mean the AI is broken. **Fact:** They're normal capacity controls; requests succeed again once usage drops or limits are raised.

#### Batch processing _(also: batch API)_

- [ ] Approved

**In a few words.** Sending lots of AI requests at once, cheaper, not instant

**Plain English.** Submitting many AI requests together to be processed in the background, at a discount, when you don't need answers right away, like scoring a thousand old tickets overnight.

**Under the hood.** Anthropic's Message Batches API processes requests asynchronously at 50% of standard prices, with most batches finishing in under an hour. It suits evaluations, backfills, and bulk analysis, not live user interactions.

**Customer line.** "For bulk jobs that can wait, batch processing cuts the AI cost in half."

**Follow-up:** _How long does a batch take?_ Most finish within an hour, but it isn't instant, so it's for background work.

**Myth:** Batch processing gives lower-quality answers because it's cheaper. **Fact:** It's the same model and quality; you trade speed of delivery for price.

#### Prompt caching

- [ ] Approved

**In a few words.** Reusing repeated prompt content at a big discount

**Plain English.** When the same long instructions or documents are sent over and over, the provider can remember them briefly so later requests are faster and much cheaper.

**Under the hood.** With prompt caching, a repeated prefix (system prompt, tools, documents) is stored for a few minutes, or longer for a fee. For most Claude models, cache reads cost about a tenth of normal input price, and cached input mostly doesn't count toward rate limits.

**Customer line.** "Prompt caching is a big reason AI costs drop at scale: repeated context gets reused at a fraction of the price."

**Follow-up:** _Does caching store our data permanently?_ No. Caches expire after minutes to an hour. Ask the vendor how it fits their retention terms.

**Myth:** Caching means the provider keeps your prompts forever. **Fact:** Prompt caches are short-lived, typically minutes to an hour.

Sources: [Anthropic: Prompt caching](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-caching)

#### Model routing _(also: model cascade)_

- [ ] Approved

> Needs a better source: the cited page is a general course, not a direct definition of this term.

**In a few words.** Sending each task to the best-fit model automatically

**Plain English.** Automatically picking which model handles each request: a fast, cheap one for simple tasks and a more capable one for hard ones.

**Under the hood.** A router (rules or a small classifier) decides per request. It can cut costs and latency, but it adds complexity, and caches don't carry across models. Anthropic's guidance suggests measuring a single capable model at lower effort before building a multi-model cascade.

**Customer line.** "Routing sends easy tasks to fast, cheap models and hard tasks to the most capable one, so you're not overpaying."

**Follow-up:** _Does routing affect quality?_ It can if the router misjudges a task, which is why routing rules should be tested like any other part of the system.

**Myth:** Using several models is always cheaper than one. **Fact:** Sometimes one capable model at a lower effort setting is simpler and just as cheap.

#### On-device AI _(also: edge AI)_

- [ ] Approved

> Needs a better source: the cited page is a general course, not a direct definition of this term.

**In a few words.** AI that runs on your phone or laptop, not the cloud

**Plain English.** AI models small enough to run directly on a phone, laptop, or local server, so data doesn't have to leave the device.

**Under the hood.** On-device models are usually small, quantized, or distilled. They offer privacy, offline use, and low latency, but are less capable than large cloud models. Many products mix both: simple tasks locally, harder ones in the cloud.

**Customer line.** "On-device AI keeps data on the device and works offline, with the tradeoff of a less powerful model."

**Follow-up:** _Is on-device AI more private?_ It can be, since data stays local, but check whether any features still send data to the cloud.

#### Latency (and time to first token) _(also: TTFT)_

- [ ] Approved

**In a few words.** How long you wait for the AI to respond

**Plain English.** How long you wait for the AI to respond. Time to first token (TTFT) is how fast the first words appear, which is what makes it feel quick.

**Under the hood.** Latency depends on model size, prompt length, output length, server load, and network. Streaming shows tokens as they're produced, so a low TTFT feels responsive even if the full answer takes longer.

**Customer line.** "Bigger models are usually smarter but slower. For live use like a support chat, speed can matter as much as raw intelligence."

**Follow-up:** _Why is it fast sometimes and slow other times?_ Longer inputs, longer answers, and busy periods on the provider's side all add time. Products often pick a faster model for real-time features.

**Myth:** A slow AI response means the answer will be better. **Fact:** Speed mostly reflects model size, workload, and length, not quality.

### Data and security

#### Data processing agreement _(also: DPA)_

- [ ] Approved

**In a few words.** The contract governing how a vendor handles your data

**Plain English.** A contract that spells out how a vendor may use, store, protect, and delete a customer's personal data. Legal and privacy teams usually require one before signing.

**Under the hood.** Under GDPR, a controller must have a contract with any processor that handles personal data on its behalf (Article 28). DPAs cover purpose limits, security measures, subprocessors, breach notification, and deletion. For AI products, buyers increasingly check whether the DPA addresses model training.

**Customer line.** "Our DPA covers exactly how your data is used and protected, including our AI providers. Happy to send it to your privacy team now."

**Follow-up:** _Does your DPA cover the AI model provider too?_ Usually the provider is listed as a subprocessor with its own terms flowing down. Confirm and share the subprocessor list.

**Myth:** A DPA is just boilerplate nobody reads. **Fact:** Privacy and legal teams read it closely, and gaps around AI can stall a deal.

Sources: [EUR-Lex: Regulation (EU) 2016/679 (GDPR)](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj)

#### Subprocessors _(also: subprocessor list)_

- [ ] Approved

**In a few words.** Other companies a vendor uses to handle your data

**Plain English.** The third parties a vendor relies on to process customer data, like its cloud host and, for AI products, its model provider. Vendors usually publish the list.

**Under the hood.** Subprocessors are bound by terms at least as protective as the vendor's own DPA. Buyers review the list, and many contracts require advance notice of new subprocessors. An AI feature often adds a model provider to the list, which can trigger review.

**Customer line.** "Here's our subprocessor list. Our AI model provider is on it, with the same data protections flowing down."

**Follow-up:** _Will you tell us if you add a new AI provider?_ Most DPAs commit to advance notice of new subprocessors. Point to the exact clause.

**Myth:** Adding an AI feature doesn't change who touches your data. **Fact:** It often adds a model provider as a new subprocessor.

Sources: [EUR-Lex: Regulation (EU) 2016/679 (GDPR)](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj)

#### Encryption in transit and at rest

- [ ] Approved

> Needs a better source: the cited page is a general course, not a direct definition of this term.

**In a few words.** Scrambling data while it moves and while it's stored

**Plain English.** Protecting data by scrambling it so only authorized systems can read it, both while it travels over the internet (in transit) and while it sits in storage (at rest).

**Under the hood.** In transit is typically TLS; at rest is typically AES-256 on disks and databases. Some enterprises also ask about customer-managed encryption keys. For AI, buyers ask whether prompts and responses are encrypted on the way to and inside the model provider.

**Customer line.** "Your data is encrypted in transit and at rest, including between us and our AI provider."

**Follow-up:** _Can we manage our own encryption keys?_ Some vendors offer customer-managed keys on enterprise plans. Check before promising it.

**Myth:** Encryption means nobody at the vendor can ever see your data. **Fact:** Encryption protects data from outsiders; access by the vendor's systems is governed by its controls and contracts.

#### SSO and SCIM _(also: single sign-on, SAML)_

- [ ] Approved

> Needs a better source: the cited page is a general course, not a direct definition of this term.

**In a few words.** Company login and automatic user provisioning

**Plain English.** Single sign-on lets employees log in with their company account (like Okta or Microsoft Entra), and SCIM automatically adds and removes users when people join or leave.

**Under the hood.** SSO usually uses SAML or OIDC. SCIM syncs user accounts from the identity provider. IT teams often require both for any tool touching company data, AI tools included, so access is cut off the day someone leaves.

**Customer line.** "It plugs into your existing login, and access is removed automatically when someone leaves the company."

**Follow-up:** _Is SSO on every plan?_ Often it's an enterprise feature. Know your pricing tiers before the IT review.

**Myth:** SSO is just a convenience feature. **Fact:** For IT, it's a security control: central login and instant offboarding.

Sources: [NIST AI Risk Management Framework](https://www.nist.gov/itl/ai-risk-management-framework)

#### Role-based access control _(also: RBAC, permissions)_

- [ ] Approved

**In a few words.** Permissions based on each person's role

**Plain English.** Giving people access based on their role, so a support agent sees support data and a manager sees reports. For AI, the key question is whether the AI respects the same permissions.

**Under the hood.** Good AI products enforce the user's permissions on everything the AI retrieves or does, so the assistant can't surface documents the user couldn't open themselves. This 'permission-aware retrieval' is a common enterprise requirement and a key defense against data leaks.

**Customer line.** "The AI only sees what each user is already allowed to see, so it can't become a back door to restricted data."

**Follow-up:** _Could the AI show someone a document they shouldn't see?_ Not if retrieval is permission-aware. Confirm how your product enforces it before answering.

**Myth:** An AI assistant needs access to everything to be useful. **Fact:** It should only reach what the user can already access; anything more is a data-leak risk.

#### Audit logs

- [ ] Approved

**In a few words.** A record of who did what, and when

**Plain English.** A tamper-resistant record of actions in a system: who logged in, what they asked the AI, and what the AI did. Security and compliance teams rely on it to investigate issues.

**Under the hood.** For AI, audit logs may capture prompts, responses, tool calls, and admin changes, which supports investigations, compliance, and accountability. There's a tension with data retention: logging more helps audits but means storing more sensitive data.

**Customer line.** "Admins can see a full record of what the AI did and who asked, which is what your compliance team will want."

**Follow-up:** _Do logs store our prompts too?_ It depends on the product and settings. Know whether logging is configurable and how long logs are kept.

**Myth:** Audit logs are only needed after something goes wrong. **Fact:** Compliance teams often require them up front as a condition of approval.

#### Data minimization

- [ ] Approved

**In a few words.** Only collecting and sending the data you actually need

**Plain English.** The principle of collecting, sending, and keeping only the personal data a task truly needs. For AI, it means not feeding the model more customer data than the job requires.

**Under the hood.** Data minimization is a core GDPR principle. In AI systems it shows up as sending only relevant fields to the model, redacting identifiers, and limiting retention. It reduces both privacy risk and token costs.

**Customer line.** "We only send the AI what it needs for the task, which protects privacy and keeps costs down."

**Follow-up:** _Doesn't more data make the AI better?_ More relevant data helps; unnecessary personal data just adds risk.

**Myth:** Giving AI all your data always improves results. **Fact:** Irrelevant data adds risk and cost without improving answers.

Sources: [EUR-Lex: Regulation (EU) 2016/679 (GDPR)](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj)

#### Redaction and masking

- [ ] Approved

**In a few words.** Hiding sensitive details before the AI sees them

**Plain English.** Automatically removing or replacing sensitive details, like names, card numbers, or health information, before data reaches the AI or gets stored.

**Under the hood.** Redaction deletes values; masking swaps them for placeholders (for example 'CUSTOMER_1') that can sometimes be restored afterward. Detection isn't perfect, so accuracy on your data types matters, and it should be tested.

**Customer line.** "Sensitive details can be masked before the AI ever sees them, and we can test how well that works on your real data."

**Follow-up:** _Does redaction catch everything?_ No tool is perfect. Ask for accuracy on your data types and decide what level of risk is acceptable.

**Myth:** Turning on redaction makes any data safe to send. **Fact:** Detection misses some cases, so it reduces risk rather than eliminating it.

**In a few words.** Running the AI in an isolated environment you control

**Plain English.** Running an AI product, or the model behind it, inside a dedicated environment such as the customer's own cloud account, instead of the vendor's shared setup.

**Under the hood.** Options include single-tenant hosting, deployment in the customer's cloud (VPC), or using a model through the customer's existing cloud provider (for example Amazon Bedrock, Google Cloud, or Microsoft Foundry for Claude). #### Shadow AI

- [ ] Approved

**In a few words.** Employees using AI tools IT hasn't approved

**Plain English.** When employees use AI tools on their own, like pasting customer data into a free chatbot, without IT or security knowing or approving.

**Under the hood.** Shadow AI creates data-leak, compliance, and quality risks because consumer tools may have different data terms and no admin controls. Many companies respond with an approved tool list, an AI acceptable use policy, and an enterprise option good enough that people don't need workarounds.

#### "Do you train on our data?"

**In a few words.** Whether customer data is used to improve the model

**Plain English.** Usually the first AI question security and legal teams ask: will our information be used to improve the vendor's model, or the model provider's?

**Under the hood.** There are two layers to check: the product vendor's policy and the policy of the model provider underneath it (for example, Anthropic, OpenAI, or a cloud platform). Business and API terms commonly say customer data isn't used for training without permission, but that's a contract term, not a law of physics. Get it in writing.

**Customer line.** "Fair question, and there are two layers to it: our policy and our model provider's. I'll send you the exact contract language for both rather than paraphrase it."

**Follow-up:** _What about the AI vendor you use under the hood?_ Name the provider, point to their commercial data terms, and confirm whether your company has a data processing agreement (DPA) with them. If you don't know, say you'll confirm. Guessing here kills trust.

#### Data retention and zero data retention (ZDR) _(also: ZDR)_

- [ ] Approved

**In a few words.** How long prompts and responses are stored, and by whom

**Plain English.** How long prompts and AI responses are stored after a request, and by whom. Zero data retention means they aren't kept after the response, apart from limited exceptions.

**Under the hood.** Retention can apply at the product layer (chat history, logs) and the model-provider layer. Providers like Anthropic offer ZDR arrangements for eligible features. #### Guardrails

- [ ] Approved

**Plain English.** Controls that keep an AI system on track and safe: what it can talk about, what data it can see, what actions it can take, and what gets blocked or flagged.

**Plain English.** A widely recognized international certification showing a company runs a proper information security program. It's common in security reviews, especially outside the US.

**Customer line.** "We're ISO 27001 certified for security. For AI-specific governance, ISO 42001 is the matching standard."

**Myth:** ISO 27001 covers AI risks. **Fact:** It covers information security broadly; ISO 42001 addresses AI management specifically.

#### NIST AI Risk Management Framework _(also: AI RMF)_

- [ ] Approved

**In a few words.** A voluntary US framework for managing AI risk

**Plain English.** A free, voluntary US framework from NIST that helps organizations spot and manage the risks of AI. It's widely used as a governance checklist.

**Under the hood.** AI RMF 1.0 was released in January 2023 and has four functions: Govern (policies and accountability), Map (understand the context and risks), Measure (test and monitor), and Manage (prioritize and respond). #### EU AI Act

- [ ] Approved

**In a few words.** The EU law that regulates AI by risk level

**Plain English.** The European Union's AI law. It sorts AI uses by risk level and puts stricter rules on riskier ones.

**Under the hood.** The Act entered into force in August 2024, with obligations phasing in over several years. It defines four tiers: unacceptable risk (banned, like social scoring), high risk (strict requirements, like AI used in hiring or critical infrastructure), limited risk (transparency duties, like telling people they're talking to a chatbot), and minimal risk (most AI, few added duties). Deadlines have been revised, so check current dates before quoting them.

**Customer line.** "Most business productivity AI falls in the lower-risk tiers, but uses like hiring decisions can be high-risk. It's worth checking where your planned use fits."

**Follow-up:** _Does it apply to us if we're a US company?_ It can, if you offer AI systems in the EU or their outputs are used there. That's a question for their legal team, but it's smart to flag it.

**Myth:** The EU AI Act bans generative AI. **Fact:** It bans a short list of specific practices and regulates others by risk level. Most generative AI use is allowed.

Sources: [EUR-Lex: Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 (AI Act)](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj)

**Under the hood.** Models improve and change price quickly, so tying a product to one model is a risk. Products often route tasks to different models (fast and cheap for simple tasks, most capable for hard ones). Buyers also care about where the model is hosted, for example through their existing cloud provider.

**Customer line.** "We pick the right model for each task and can switch as the market moves, so you get the improvements without migrating anything."

**Follow-up:** _Can we bring our own model or use our cloud provider's?_ Some products support this and some don't. Check with your product team, because it's a common enterprise requirement.

**Myth:** The product that uses the newest, biggest model is always the best. **Fact:** Fit matters more: accuracy on your task, speed, cost, and data terms.

#### AI washing

- [ ] Approved

**In a few words.** Overstating how much AI a product really uses

**Plain English.** Overstating how much AI a product uses, or what it can do, for marketing. Buyers are increasingly skeptical because of it.

**Under the hood.** Regulators have acted on misleading AI claims using existing rules: in March 2024 the SEC settled charges against two investment advisers (Delphia and Global Predictions) for overstating their AI use, and the FTC launched an enforcement sweep called Operation AI Comply in September 2024. Buyers respond by asking for demos on their own data, evals, and specifics about what's actually AI and what's rules or human work.

**Customer line.** "I'd rather show you than tell you. Let's run it on your real examples and you can judge what the AI is actually doing."

**Follow-up:** _How much of your product is actually AI?_ Be specific: which features use a model, which use rules, and where people are involved. Precise answers build credibility fast.

**Myth:** Saying 'AI-powered' in every pitch helps you win more deals. **Fact:** Vague claims now raise skepticism. Specific, provable claims win.

**Plain English.** How long it takes after signing for a customer to get meaningful results. For AI products, a quick first win builds the trust needed for wider rollout.

**Under the hood.** AI time to value depends on integrations, data readiness, security approval, and user training. Starting with one high-volume use case and a measured baseline usually gets a provable win fastest, which then funds expansion.

**In a few words.** Paying per result, like per resolved ticket

**Plain English.** Pricing AI by the results it delivers, such as per conversation resolved or per task completed, instead of per user or per token.

**Plain English.** The full cost of an AI solution over its life: license or usage fees, plus implementation, integration, training, security reviews, maintenance, and staff time.

#### Usage-based (token) pricing vs seat pricing

- [ ] Approved

**In a few words.** Paying per use of AI versus paying per user

**Plain English.** AI model providers usually charge by usage (tokens in and tokens out). SaaS products have traditionally charged per user (seat). Many AI products now mix the two.

**Under the hood.** Model APIs price input and output tokens separately, and output usually costs more. Costs can be lowered with prompt caching (reusing repeated input at a discount), batch processing (cheaper, not real-time), and choosing smaller models where they're good enough. Vendors may pass this through as credits, usage tiers, or caps on top of seats.

**Customer line.** "Seats are predictable. Usage scales with value. We can structure it so you have a predictable budget with room to grow."

**Follow-up:** _How do we avoid a surprise bill?_ Ask about spending caps, usage alerts, and admin dashboards. A good vendor will help estimate usage from a pilot.

**Myth:** AI usage costs are basically zero once you've paid for the license. **Fact:** Every AI request has a real computing cost, which is why AI features often come with usage limits or credits.

**In a few words.** Proving the AI is worth what it costs

**Plain English.** Showing that the AI is worth what it costs, in time saved, revenue gained, risk reduced, or quality improved.

**Under the hood.** Strong AI ROI cases measure a baseline first (time per task, error rate, volume), run a pilot on real work, and compare. Time saved only counts if it goes somewhere useful. Adoption rate is often the deciding factor, since unused seats deliver nothing.

#### False positives and false negatives _(also: precision and recall)_

- [ ] Approved

**In a few words.** Flagging something harmless versus missing something real

**Plain English.** A false positive is when the AI flags something that turns out to be nothing, like a real email sent to spam. A false negative is something real it missed, like a spam email that lands in your inbox. Every AI system that flags, filters, or scores things trades one against the other.

**Under the hood.** Precision is the share of flagged items that are truly relevant (few false positives). Recall is the share of truly relevant items that got flagged (few false negatives). Tuning a threshold usually improves one at the expense of the other. The right balance depends on which mistake costs more.

**Customer line.** "No AI gets every call right, so the real question is which mistake costs you more: flagging too much, or missing something important. We can tune it toward whichever one matters more to your team."

**Follow-up:** _Can't you just get both to zero?_ No real system does. Anyone claiming zero false positives and zero misses is overselling. What matters is a balance that fits the cost of each mistake.

**Myth:** A higher accuracy percentage always means a better system. **Fact:** If real events are rare, a system can be 99% 'accurate' while missing most of them. Precision and recall tell you more.

Sources: [Anthropic: Define success criteria and build evaluations](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/test-and-evaluate/develop-tests)
